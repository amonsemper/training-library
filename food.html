Food Storage

Building a food supply you'll actually use: how much to keep, a pantry that rotates itself, long-term staples that last decades, what to do with the fridge and freezer when the power goes out, and cooking without the grid.

This is a reference, not food-safety certification. When in doubt, throw it out: food poisoning in an emergency, with no doctor and no clean water, is far worse than going hungry for a meal. Follow USDA guidance on home canning; untested methods can cause botulism.

01How much to keep

Ready.gov's minimum is three days of food per person; two weeks covers most outages and disruptions, and many families build from there to one and then three months.

Count calories, not cans

Most adults need around 2,000 calories a day, more when working hard or in cold weather (2,500–3,000+). A pantry that looks full can hold surprisingly few calories.

Build in layers

Start with two weeks of what you already eat, then add long-term staples. Each layer covers a longer disruption without anything going to waste.

Special needs first

Infant formula, medical diets, food allergies, prescriptions taken with food, and pet food. These are the hardest to find during a shortage.

Food you'll eat

Stress isn't the time to discover you hate the bucket of survival food. Store what your household already eats and knows how to cook.

02The rotating pantry

The simplest system: store what you eat, eat what you store. Buy extra of the shelf-stable things you use every week, and always use the oldest first.

First in, first out

New purchases go to the back, old ones move to the front. Write the purchase month on each item with a marker.

Good pantry foods

Canned meat, fish, beans, soup, fruit and vegetables; peanut butter; pasta and rice; oats; crackers; dried fruit and nuts; powdered milk; honey; coffee and tea.

Needs no cooking

Keep some food that's ready to eat cold: canned meat and beans, nut butters, crackers, bars. You may not always be able to cook.

The forgotten items

A manual can opener (two), salt, cooking oil, spices, vitamins, and comfort food. Morale matters in a long disruption.

03Long-term staples

Dry staples with very little moisture or fat last for decades when packed properly. They're cheap per calorie and make the backbone of a long-term supply.

StapleWhyNotes
White riceCheap calories, long lifeBrown rice goes rancid in months to a year because of its oils.
Dry beans and lentilsProtein, pairs with riceOld beans take much longer to cook; budget more fuel and water.
Wheat berries, rolled oatsBreakfast and bakingWheat needs a grain mill to make flour. Flour itself keeps far less time.
PastaEasy, familiarCooks fast, so it saves fuel.
Sugar, honey, saltKeep indefinitelyStore dry. Don't pack sugar or salt with oxygen absorbers: they harden into a brick.
Freeze-dried mealsLightweight, just add waterExpensive, but very long shelf life and easy for a go-bag or shelter.

04Shelf life

Typical figures for cool (around 70 °F or below), dry, dark storage. Heat is the biggest enemy: as a rule of thumb, every 10 °F warmer roughly halves shelf life. A hot garage ruins food storage.

FoodTypical shelf life
High-acid canned food (tomatoes, fruit, juice)12–18 months
Low-acid canned food (meat, vegetables, soup, beans)2–5 years
Peanut butter, unopened6–24 months
Rice, pasta, oats, beans in original packaging1–2 years
White rice, dry beans, oats, wheat sealed in Mylar with oxygen absorbersUp to 25–30 years
Freeze-dried meals (unopened, per maker)Up to 25–30 years
Powdered milk (nonfat, sealed)Up to 20 years sealed; months once opened

Dates on cans are quality dates, not safety dates. Throw out any can that is bulging, leaking, badly dented on a seam, rusted through, or that spurts when opened.

05Packing for the long haul

Mylar bags + oxygen absorbers

Fill the bag, drop in the right size absorber, squeeze the air out and seal it with an iron or impulse sealer. Store the bags inside food-grade buckets so rodents can't chew through.

Buckets and gamma lids

Food-grade 5-gallon buckets with screw-on gamma lids make bulk dry goods easy to open and close every week.

Only dry food

Only pack foods with low moisture (about 10% or less) and low fat in oxygen-free containers. Moist foods sealed without oxygen are where botulism grows.

Off the floor, out of the heat

Keep buckets on shelves or pallets, away from walls that sweat, out of the garage and attic, and away from chemicals and fuel.

06Fridge and freezer in an outage

Keep the doors shut

An unopened fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours (a half-full one about 24) if you don't open it.

The 40 °F rule

Throw out perishable food (meat, dairy, eggs, leftovers) that has been above 40 °F for 2 hours or more. A cheap appliance thermometer in each tells you instead of guessing.

Refreezing

Food in the freezer that still has ice crystals or is at 40 °F or below can be refrozen safely.

Eat in order

Fridge food first, then the freezer, then the pantry. A generator or power station running the fridge in cycles (see Off-Grid Configurations) stretches this a long way.

07Cooking without power

Camp stove

Propane or butane. Outdoors or in a well-ventilated space only: they make carbon monoxide. Keep spare fuel canisters.

Grill

Gas or charcoal grills outdoors only, never in a garage, tent, or under an awning that traps fumes.

Fuel-saving tricks

Soak beans overnight, use a lid, and try "retained heat" cooking: bring rice or beans to a boil, then wrap the pot in blankets or put it in a cooler to finish cooking without fuel.

Wood

A wood stove, fireplace with a cooking grate, or an outdoor rocket stove. See Fire and Shelter.
