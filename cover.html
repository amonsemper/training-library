Cover and Concealment

Cover stops the rounds being fired at you. Concealment only hides you. Both are useful, they aren't interchangeable, and confusing the two is one of the most common and costly mistakes in a gunfight. This page collects the definitions, what common materials actually stop, and how to use cover indoors, around vehicles, and on open ground, whatever you're carrying.

Built on U.S. Army soldier common-task doctrine (TC 3-21.75) and common defensive and law-enforcement training. Material ratings are general guidance, not test data: construction, angle, and the specific round all change the result. When in doubt, treat it as concealment.

01Cover vs. concealment

Cover

Something solid enough to stop incoming fire: an engine block, a large tree, earth, thick masonry. Fight from behind it, reload behind it, fix malfunctions behind it, or wait behind it for help. It's temporary: repeated hits wear it down, and an attacker can move to a new angle.

Concealment

Something that keeps you from being seen but won't stop a bullet: drywall, doors, car doors, hedges, furniture, darkness, smoke. It still helps, because you can't be aimed at if you can't be seen. Use it to move, reposition, or break contact. Expect fire through it at where you were last seen.

Cover depends on the threat's weapon, not yours. A brick wall that stops a 9mm may give way to a rifle after a few hits. Judge every position against what's being fired at you.

03Principles

Stand back from it

A few feet back lets you see more around the edge with less of your body showing, and keeps your muzzle from sticking out past the edge where it can be seen or grabbed. Three to six feet when space allows.

Don't hug hard surfaces

A bullet that hits a wall, a car, or the ground at a shallow angle tends to skip along it a few inches off the surface, straight into someone pressed against it.

Cover is directional

A wall protects you from one direction only. Know where the threat is, and expect it to move to an angle where your cover no longer helps.

Cover buys time

Use it for the things that take time and attention: reloading, clearing a malfunction, calling for help, treating a wound, deciding your next move.

04Shooting from cover

  • Work the edge in small steps (slicing the pie), so you see the threat a little at a time instead of stepping out into the open.
  • Shoot around the side rather than over the top when you can; it exposes less of your head.
  • Get low behind low cover. Kneeling or prone behind an engine block or a berm hides far more of you than standing.
  • Mind the mechanical offset with a long gun. The optic sits above the bore: the dot can be clear of a low edge while the barrel is still behind it, and the round hits your own cover.
  • Switch shoulders or hands to match the side of cover if you've trained it. It cuts exposure on the support-side corner.
  • Don't stay in one spot longer than you have to. Once the threat knows where you are, change position or angle when you can.

05Moving between cover

  • Know where you're going before you leave, and the route to it.
  • Move in short rushes. The Army's rule of thumb is about three to five seconds in the open: "I'm up, he sees me, I'm down."
  • Reload before you move, not halfway across.
  • Use concealment along the way to hide the movement, and come up somewhere other than where you went down.
  • In a group, one moves while another covers. Say it out loud: "Moving," "Move."

06Indoors

Inside most homes and offices, almost nothing is cover. Interior walls, doors, and furniture are concealment, and rounds fired inside pass through several rooms.

  • Position matters more than walls. Pick a spot where a threat has to come to you through a doorway or hallway, ideally with something solid behind them.
  • Kitchens and bathrooms may have appliances, cabinets, and tile that slow rounds, but don't rely on them.
  • Know what's behind your target. Your own rounds pass through the same walls; family members' rooms, neighbors, and the street are all downrange.
  • A long barrel crowds doorways. Stay back from the frame with a rifle or shotgun so the muzzle doesn't lead you around the corner.

07Vehicles

Where the cover is

  • The engine block, and the wheels and axles.
  • Everything else, doors, body panels, seats, and glass, is concealment.

Using it

  • Get low behind the front wheel and engine.
  • Keep your feet behind a wheel; rounds fired under the car hit ankles and feet.
  • Stay a few feet back from the body; rounds skip along it.
  • Glass deflects bullets unpredictably, both coming in and going out.

08Open ground

  • Earth is the best cover outdoors: a ditch, a berm, a dip in the ground, a road embankment. Prone behind a fold of dirt beats standing behind a fence.
  • Large trees and stone walls are cover; brush, fences, and sheet-metal outbuildings are not.
  • Hay bales stacked deep slow handgun rounds but don't reliably stop rifle rounds.
  • Low ground can hide you completely; learn to see the dead space a threat can't shoot into.

09Using concealment

  • Break line of sight to move. A threat that can't see you can't aim at you, and has to guess where you'll reappear.
  • Darkness is concealment. Use light only when you need it, then move; the light shows where you were.
  • Don't stay behind concealment once you've been seen. Expect rounds through it at that spot.
  • Concealment is not a firing position. If you have to shoot from behind it, move afterward.

10Common errors

  • Treating car doors, interior walls, or fences as cover.
  • Hugging cover, which exposes you to skipping rounds and pushes the muzzle past the edge.
  • Staying in one spot after the threat knows where you are.
  • Leaving cover without knowing where you're going next.
  • Reloading or fixing a malfunction in the open when cover is a few steps away.
  • Forgetting that cover against a handgun may not be cover against a rifle.
