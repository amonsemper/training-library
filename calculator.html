Ballistics Calculator

Drop, holds in MOA and mils, wind drift, velocity, energy, and time of flight for 33 cartridges and more than 100 common loads: rimfire, handgun and pistol-caliber carbine, intermediate rifle, hunting and long-range rifle, lever-action and straight-wall, and shotgun shot, buckshot, and slugs.

A point-mass solver using the standard G1 drag model. Load data are typical published figures; your barrel length, ammunition lot, and conditions change them. Chronograph your own gun and confirm every hold on paper before you rely on it.

02How to use it

  1. Pick the cartridge, then the load closest to what you shoot. The fields fill in with typical values.
  2. Replace the muzzle velocity with your own chronograph reading. It matters more than any other input.
  3. Measure your sight height: the distance from the center of the bore to the center of the sight or optic.
  4. Set your zero distance, your temperature and altitude, and the wind.
  5. Type a distance into "Hold at" for the exact elevation and wind correction there. The table and chart update as you type.
  6. Shoot at real distances and adjust the velocity until the calculator matches your hits. That's called truing.

03What the inputs mean

InputWhat it isEffect
Bullet weightIn grains (7,000 per pound)Used for energy. Drop comes from velocity and BC.
Muzzle velocitySpeed leaving the barrel, in ft/sThe biggest single factor in drop. Shorter barrels are slower.
G1 BCBallistic coefficient: how well the bullet keeps its speed against air dragHigher BC means less drop and drift at long range.
Sight heightBore center to sight center, in inchesMatters most at close range and for the near zero distance. About 2.6" on an AR, 1.5–2" on a scoped bolt gun, 0.5–0.6" on a handgun.
ZeroThe distance where the bullet crosses the line of sightSets where you're on, and how high the bullet rises between.
WindSpeed in mph and the clock direction it comes from3 and 9 o'clock are full value; 12 and 6 have no drift.
Temperature and altitudeAir densityWarmer, higher air is thinner: less drop and drift.

04Limits

  • G1 vs. G7. Long, boat-tailed match bullets are better described by the G7 model. Past about 800 yards, G1 figures can be off by several inches; use the bullet maker's own calculator for long-range work.
  • Shot and buckshot are plotted as one pellet with an estimated drag figure for a round ball. Use the result for drop and retained energy, not precision. Patterning your gun matters more.
  • Not modeled: spin drift, Coriolis effect, shooting at steep angles, and the transonic instability some bullets show as they slow through the speed of sound.
  • Subsonic loads (.300 and 8.6 Blackout, standard-velocity .22 LR) drop steeply past 100 yards. The calculator shows it, but small range errors become large misses.
