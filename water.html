Water

Water runs out first and matters most: how much to store, how to store it so it stays safe, how to make questionable water drinkable, where the water already hiding in your house is, and how to handle sanitation when the taps stop.

This is a reference, not public-health advice. When your local water authority issues a boil or do-not-drink notice, follow it. No home method removes chemical contamination such as fuel, pesticides, or industrial runoff: if water may be chemically contaminated, don't drink it, whatever you do to it.

01How much you need

The standard planning figure is one gallon per person per day: about half a gallon to drink and the rest for food preparation and basic hygiene. That's a minimum, not a comfortable amount.

Store at least two weeks

Ready.gov's floor is three days; two weeks covers most real outages, boil notices, and storm recoveries. For a family of four that's 56 gallons.

Plan for more

Hot weather can double what people need. So can heavy work, illness, pregnancy, and nursing. Children and older adults dehydrate faster.

Don't forget pets

Dogs and cats need water too; a medium dog drinks roughly a quart or more a day, more in heat. Count them in the total.

Drink it, don't ration it

Drink what your body needs today and look for more tomorrow. Rationing water while it's still available causes dehydration that impairs judgment exactly when you need it.

02Storing water

ContainerGood forNotes
Commercially bottled waterEasiest startSealed and safe until opened. Keep it in its original packaging; rotate by the date on it.
Food-grade water jugs (2–7 gal)Most householdsLook for HDPE plastic marked #2 and sold for drinking water. Portable, stackable, easy to rotate.
Food-grade barrels (30–55 gal)Large volumes at homeToo heavy to move once full (a 55-gal barrel weighs about 460 lb). Store on wood or a pallet, not bare concrete, and keep a siphon or barrel pump with it.
Bathtub bladder (WaterBOB-type)Short notice before a stormFill the tub with a liner bag when a storm is forecast. Holds 60–100 gallons for the following days.

Don't use

Milk and juice jugs (they're hard to clean and break down within months), containers that held chemicals, and anything not made for food.

Fill and seal it right

Wash containers with soap and water, rinse, sanitize with a teaspoon of unscented bleach per quart of water, rinse again, then fill from a safe tap. Municipal tap water already contains chlorine; well water should be treated as in section 04.

Where to keep it

Cool, dark, and away from gasoline, paint, pesticides, and solvents: plastic lets vapors through. Not in direct sun, which warms it and grows algae.

Rotate it

Replace home-filled water every six months (an easy rule: when the clocks change). Write the fill date on every container.

03Making water safe

Any water that isn't sealed bottled water or stored tap water should be treated before drinking, brushing teeth, washing food, or making baby formula.

Boiling (best)Bacteria, viruses, protozoaChemicals, sedimentBring to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft). Let it cool covered.

Cloudy water: let it settle, then pour the clear water off or strain it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter before any treatment. Sediment shields germs from chlorine and UV and clogs filters.

04Bleach dosing

Use regular, unscented chlorine bleach with no additives (not "splash-less", scented, or color-safe), and check the label for its strength. Bleach loses strength over time; use a bottle less than a year old.

1 quart / 1 liter2 drops2 drops
1 gallon8 drops (about 1/8 tsp)6 drops
2 gallons16 drops (1/4 tsp)12 drops (1/8 tsp)

Stir, then wait 30 minutes. The water should have a slight chlorine smell. If it doesn't, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. If the water was cloudy and you couldn't clear it, EPA guidance is to double the dose.

05Filters and tablets

Personal straw or squeeze filter

Light and cheap (Sawyer Squeeze, LifeStraw). Good in a go-bag. Filters bacteria and protozoa, not viruses or chemicals. Don't let a used hollow-fiber filter freeze: ice cracks the fibers invisibly.

Gravity filter

A dirty bag hung above a clean one, or a countertop unit (Berkey-type). Filters gallons without pumping; good for a household. Check what the specific model is certified to remove.

Tablets

Chlorine dioxide tablets (Katadyn Micropur, Aquamira) weigh almost nothing and keep for years. Iodine tablets don't kill Cryptosporidium and aren't for pregnant women or people with thyroid problems.

Belt and suspenders

For water from floods, ditches, or near livestock: settle, filter, then treat chemically or boil. Each step covers what the others miss.

06Water already in your house

Water heater (40–80 gal)

Turn off the power or gas first. Close the cold supply valve, open a hot tap upstairs to let air in, and drain from the valve at the bottom. The first water out may carry sediment: let it settle. Don't turn the heater back on until it's full again.

Pipes

Shut the main valve so contaminated water can't flow back in, open the highest faucet in the house, then collect from the lowest one.

Toilet tank (not the bowl)

The tank behind the bowl holds clean supply water, unless you use in-tank cleaning tablets. Treat it before drinking.

Ice and canned food

Melted ice cubes and the liquid in canned fruit and vegetables count.

Swimming pools and hot tubs: use for flushing toilets and washing, not drinking. Pool chemicals aren't safe to drink and boiling concentrates them.

07Sources outside

Rain

A clean tarp or a gutter downspout into a covered, food-grade barrel. Divert the first few minutes of rain ("first flush"), which carries roof dirt and droppings. Treat before drinking.

Streams, lakes, springs

Moving water is better than still. Collect upstream of roads, farms, and towns where you can. Always treat: clear water still carries Giardia and bacteria.

Floodwater

Avoid it. It's mixed with sewage, fuel, and chemicals that no home method removes.

Carry capacity

Water weighs 8.3 lb per gallon. A collapsible jug or a few clean buckets with lids make a trip worth the effort.

08Sanitation without running water

Disease from poor sanitation kills more people after disasters than the disaster itself. Plan this before you need it.

Flushing with a bucket

If the sewer still works, pour a gallon or two of non-drinking water quickly into the bowl to flush. Don't do this if sewers are backing up.

Bucket toilet

A 5-gallon bucket lined with heavy trash bags, a toilet seat lid made for it, and something absorbent to cover each use (cat litter, sawdust, or peat). Seal and store bags outside away from water sources.

Hand washing

Keep a jug with a spigot, soap, and a catch basin by the bucket toilet and the kitchen. Hand sanitizer is a backup, not a replacement, when hands are visibly dirty.

Disinfecting surfaces

For surfaces (not drinking water), about 1/3 cup of unscented bleach per gallon of water. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.
