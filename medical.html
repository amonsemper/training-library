Field Medical

Keeping a hurt person alive until real medical care takes over: working in the right order, stopping bleeding that kills in minutes, managing the airway and chest, carrying a kit you can reach, handling an unresponsive adult or child, protecting a casualty from cold, and moving them without making things worse.

This is a reference, not training, and it is not medical advice. None of it replaces a hands-on course and a provider's judgment. Take a Stop the Bleed class, then a TCCC-based or wilderness first-aid course, practice with your own kit, and call emergency services the moment you can. In an emergency, do what a dispatcher or a more qualified person on scene tells you.

01Work in order: MARCH

Most people who die from trauma before reaching a hospital die of bleeding, and most of that is survivable with simple actions taken fast. The hard part under stress is doing things in the order that saves the most lives, instead of fixing the first injury you see. The military sequence MARCH is the civilian's best shortcut: it puts the fastest killers first.

Before any of it, make the scene safe. You are no use as a second casualty: stop the threat, move out of a line of fire or off a road, and put on gloves if you have them. Then move down the letters.

Say the letters out loud if it helps. The order is the whole point: a sealed chest does no good if an arm is still pumping blood onto the ground.

02Massive hemorrhage

Life-threatening bleeding is blood that spurts, pools, soaks clothing fast, or won't stop. You have minutes. Which tool you reach for depends on where the wound is: a limb, or somewhere a tourniquet can't go.

Tourniquet (arms and legs)

For serious bleeding from an arm or leg, a tourniquet is first, not last. Put it high and tight on the limb, above the wound, over bare skin where you can. Tighten until the bleeding stops and the limb goes still; it will hurt, which is normal. If one doesn't stop it, add a second just above the first. Write the time you applied it on the casualty or the tourniquet. Once it's on, leave it on for help to manage. Use a real windlass tourniquet (CAT or SOF-T); improvised ones rarely work.

Dirty Civilian: Massive hemorrhage and tourniquets8:10

Wound packing (where a tourniquet can't go)

For deep bleeding at the groin, armpit, neck, or a limb junction, pack the wound. Push hemostatic gauze (or plain gauze, or clean cloth) straight into the wound, right down onto the spot that's bleeding, and keep stuffing it in tightly until the wound is full. Then hold firm pressure for at least three minutes without peeking. Pack over the bleeder, not just into the hole.

Dirty Civilian: Wound packing, QuikClot, and bandaging26:38

Direct pressure and bundling

Direct pressure stops or slows almost anything while you set up. Press hard, with your whole weight through a gloved hand or a wad of gauze, right on the wound. Don't trade a working press for a loose wrap. When you do dress it, a pressure bandage holds the packing in and frees your hands.

Garand Thumb: Stop the Bleed (ACS): direct pressure0:24

What not to do

Don't waste time looking for a pressure point or elevating a limb instead of packing and pressing. Don't loosen a tourniquet to "let blood through." Don't pour hemostatic powder into a deep wound where you can't pack it. Don't assume a small entry wound is the whole story: look for an exit and for blood pooling under the body.

03Junctional and chest wounds

Some bleeding is in a place you can neither tourniquet nor fully pack. These are the wounds that kill fast and need rapid evacuation above all else.

Junctional wounds

The groin, the armpit, the base of the neck: a limb joins the trunk and a big vessel runs shallow. A limb tourniquet won't reach. Pack hard with hemostatic gauze and hold deep, firm pressure, aiming your push toward the bleeding vessel. For a neck wound, do not wrap anything all the way around the neck. These casualties need a surgeon soon.

Dirty Civilian: Junctional wounds: groin, armpit, and neck7:51

Open chest wounds

A hole in the chest can let air into the space around the lung and collapse it. Cover any open chest wound with a vented chest seal (a plain seal, or even tape sealed on three sides, if that's all you have). Watch for breathing that gets worse after sealing: if it does, lift a corner of the seal for a second to let trapped air out, then reseal.

Garand Thumb: Chest seals in an IFAK6:19

Bleeding you can't see or reach, inside the chest, belly, or pelvis, is not something you fix on scene. Control what you can from the outside, keep the casualty still and warm, and get them to a hospital as fast as is safe.

04Airway and breathing

Once the bleeding is handled, make sure air is moving. An unconscious person's biggest airway threat is their own tongue and their own vomit.

The breathing but unconscious casualty

If they're breathing on their own but out cold and you don't suspect a spine injury that rules it out, roll them onto their side in the recovery position. It keeps the tongue off the airway and lets fluid drain instead of choking them. Recheck that they keep breathing.

Nasal airway

A nasopharyngeal airway (a soft lubricated tube through the nose) holds the airway open on someone who's unconscious but breathing and tolerates it. It's a trained skill and a kit item, not a guess; learn it before you carry it.

05The IFAK

An individual first-aid kit is for the fast killers, carried where you can reach it with either hand. Keep it separate from your "boo-boo" kit of blister pads and painkillers: when you need the IFAK, you need it now, and you don't want to be digging past band-aids.

Buy name-brand, in-date components, not a bargain pouch of look-alikes. Know where every item is by feel. A kit you've never opened is a kit you can't use under stress.

06Unresponsive and choking

These are the medical emergencies, as opposed to trauma, most likely to land in front of you: someone collapses, or someone can't breathe at the table. Current guidance changed recently, so check the date on whatever you were taught.

Unresponsive, not breathing: CPR

No response and no normal breathing means start CPR and get someone calling emergency services and finding an AED. Push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about two inches deep for an adult, letting the chest come all the way back up between pushes, around 100 to 120 a minute. Don't stop for long. Use the AED the moment it arrives and follow its voice prompts.

Dirty Civilian: Unresponsive adult and CPR38:15

Infants and small children

For an infant, current guidance is to compress with two thumbs (hands wrapping the chest) or the heel of one hand, not two fingers, about a third of the depth of the chest. Children and infants far more often stop breathing from an airway or breathing problem than from the heart, so rescue breaths matter: take a child or infant CPR class, because the technique differs from an adult's.

Dirty Civilian: Unresponsive infant CPR58:35

Choking

If a choking adult or child is conscious and can't cough, speak, or breathe, current guidance is to alternate five back blows between the shoulder blades with five abdominal thrusts, and keep repeating until the object comes out or they go unconscious. For an infant, alternate five back blows with five chest thrusts using the heel of one hand; do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant. If they go limp, lower them down, call emergency services, and start CPR.

Dirty Civilian: Choking infant1:08:04

The specifics above reflect the 2025 CPR and first-aid guidance, which added back blows for choking and changed infant technique. Guidance is revised periodically. A current, hands-on CPR course is the only way to actually be ready; reading it is not.

07Cold and heat

Weather finishes casualties that the injury alone wouldn't. A hurt, bleeding, still person loses heat fast, even in mild conditions, and cold blood clots poorly, which makes the bleeding worse.

Hypothermia

Treat every serious trauma casualty as a hypothermia risk: get them off the cold ground, strip wet clothing, and wrap them in whatever holds heat, head included. Shivering, fumbling, and slurred speech are early signs; as it deepens, shivering stops and they get confused and drowsy. Rewarm gently, add heat at the core and armpits, and handle them carefully, because a very cold heart is easy to tip into a bad rhythm.

Dirty Civilian: Hypothermia: the stages26:55Dirty Civilian: Hypothermia: treatment and rewarming34:51

Heat illness

Heat exhaustion is heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, and cramps: move them to shade, cool them, and give water if they're fully alert. Heat stroke, where they stop making sense, stop sweating, and run hot, is a true emergency: cool them aggressively, ideally in cold water, and get help now. Don't wait to see if it passes.

08Moving and handing off

Treat what kills in place first, then move. Moving a casualty is covered in detail on the Fieldcraft page: the drags, carries, and improvised litters, and when the move is worth the cost.

  • Finish your bleeding control before you move anyone; a carry that undoes a tourniquet or a pack is a step backward.
    Dirty Civilian: Evacuating wounded: before transport2:15
  • Keep a possible spine injury in line if the situation lets you, but don't let fear of moving someone leave them in a fire or a flooding car.
  • Write it down: the time each tourniquet went on, what you gave, how they've changed. The next provider will ask.
  • Hand off cleanly. Tell arriving help what happened, what you found, and what you did, in that order, then get out of their way.

09Train for real

Everything here is muscle memory you don't have until you've done it with your hands, under a clock, on a mannequin and a training tourniquet. Build it in this order:

  • Stop the Bleed. A short, widely available class on tourniquets, packing, and pressure. Start here.
    Garand Thumb: How to Stop the Bleed (ACS)0:00
  • CPR / AED. A current certification course, with a separate infant and child class if you have kids around.
  • TCCC-based or wilderness first aid. The next step up: MARCH, airway, chest, and working away from help.
    Dirty Civilian: Becoming a tactical medic, with a Navy corpsman0:00
  • Practice your own kit. Open it, lay it out, and run the motions until you can find each item blind. Replace what expires.
