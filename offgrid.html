Off-Grid Environment Configurations

Keeping a home running when the grid is down: knowing your loads, battery backup from a portable power station to a whole-home system, portable and standby generators, the fuels that run them and how long each keeps, connecting it all to the house safely, recharging from solar, heating and cooling without the grid, and four configurations from a single room to a fully off-grid property.

This is a reference, not electrical or fire-code advice. Connecting a generator or battery to house wiring is work for a licensed electrician and usually needs a permit. Generators make carbon monoxide, which kills quietly: never run one indoors, in a garage, or near a window, door, or vent, and keep battery-powered CO alarms in the house. Check your local fire code before storing fuel.

01Three numbers

Every backup power decision comes down to three numbers about the things you need to keep running. Work them out first, then buy equipment to match. Buying a generator or battery first and hoping it covers the house is how people end up with a freezer that won't start or a battery that's flat by midnight.

Running watts

What everything you want on at the same time draws while it's running. This sets the continuous output your inverter or generator needs.

Starting (surge) watts

Motors and compressors (refrigerators, freezers, well and sump pumps, air conditioners) draw two to three times their running watts for a second or two as they start. Your source has to carry everything already running plus the biggest motor starting.

Energy per day

Watts multiplied by hours, in kilowatt-hours (kWh). A 100 W load for 10 hours is 1 kWh. This sets battery size and how much fuel you burn. A typical U.S. home uses around 29 kWh a day; a backup plan usually covers a fraction of that.

Cut the list

The cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one you don't need. Decide what's critical (refrigeration, water, heat, medical devices, communications, a few lights) and what waits for the grid (electric heat, water heater, dryer, oven, central air).

03Battery backup

Battery power stations, often sold as "solar generators" when they're bundled with panels, have two big advantages over a fuel generator: they're silent, and they need no fuel. No exhaust, so they can run indoors and right next to a bed; no fuel to buy, store, rotate, or run out of; no engine to start or maintain; and nothing to hear from outside the house. Paired with solar, they refill themselves every sunny day. The trade-off is that they store a fixed amount of energy: when it's gone, you need sun, a generator, or the grid to refill it.

TypeTypical capacityOutputBest for
Portable power station0.5–5 kWhBuilt-in AC outlets, USB and 12 V; 300–3,600 WPhones, radios, CPAP, router, lights, a fridge for part of a day. Plug-and-play, no installation.
Whole-home battery systemAbout 10–15 kWh per unit, stackableWired into the house panel; typically 5–11+ kW continuousCritical-load panels or the whole house, switching over automatically, usually paired with solar.
12 V DC system (DIY or kit)1–10+ kWh of LiFePO4Fused 12 V DC plus an inverter if neededRadio stations, lighting, and small loads; vehicles, sheds and cabins.

Chemistry

LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate)

The usual choice for home backup now. Thousands of charge cycles, most of the capacity usable, stable and hard to set on fire compared with other lithium types. Most can't safely charge below freezing unless they have built-in heaters: keep them inside, or buy a self-heating model.

NMC lithium

Lighter for its capacity, so common in smaller portables, but fewer cycles and less tolerant of heat and abuse than LiFePO4.

Lead-acid (AGM, flooded)

Cheap and familiar, but heavy, and you should only use about half the rated capacity or the battery wears out fast. Flooded batteries vent hydrogen while charging and need ventilation and topping up.

Sizing rule

Battery kWh needed is roughly your daily kWh times the number of days, divided by about 0.8 to cover inverter losses and keep some reserve. The estimator above does this for you.

Common brands

These are the brands you'll see most on Amazon and in the big-box stores. Every one makes LiFePO4 models, sells matching solar panels, and has a range from small to home-backup size. What to compare

Capacity in watt-hours (how long it runs), continuous and surge output in watts (what it can start and run), LiFePO4 chemistry and rated cycle count, solar input in watts and its voltage range, and whether it can add expansion batteries.

For home backup

Look for a UPS or pass-through mode (switches over in milliseconds when the grid drops), 120/240 V output if you need a well pump or other 240 V load, and a transfer switch or home panel kit from the same maker for hardwired circuits.

04Generators

A generator turns fuel into power for as long as you can feed it, so it covers long outages and heavy loads that a battery can't. The trade-offs are noise, exhaust, fuel storage, and maintenance.

TypeTypical outputStrengthsWeaknesses
Inverter portable1,000–7,000 WQuiet, fuel-efficient at light load (it throttles down), clean power that's safe for electronics, often parallel-capableCosts more per watt; smaller units can't start a well pump or central air
Open-frame portable3,000–12,000 WLots of watts for the money; can start big motorsLoud, runs at full speed whatever the load, so it burns more fuel; rougher power on some models
Home standby10–26+ kWPermanently installed, runs on natural gas or propane, starts itself with an automatic transfer switch, exercises itself weeklyExpensive, needs permits and professional installation, and depends on a gas supply or a large propane tank

Sizing

Pick a generator whose running watts cover your running load with some headroom (plan to use no more than about 80% continuously), and whose starting watts cover the running load plus your largest motor starting.

Dual and tri-fuel

Many portables run on gasoline and propane, and some on natural gas as well. Expect roughly 10–20% less output on propane or natural gas than on gasoline. Being able to switch fuels is worth a lot in a long outage.

Run it less

A generator burns fuel even at light load. Pairing it with a battery lets you run the generator hard for a few hours to charge the battery and run big loads, then shut it off and run quietly on the battery. That saves fuel, noise, and engine hours.

Fuel burn

A portable generator at half load typically burns somewhere around 0.3–0.8 gallons of gasoline an hour depending on size. Check your model's runtime figures and plan fuel from them, not from a guess.

05Fuel types

The fuel decides how long you can keep going, how you store it, and what happens in cold weather. Approximate energy content is per U.S. gallon (natural gas per 100 cubic feet).

Rotate, don't hoard

Use stored gasoline and diesel in vehicles and replace it on a schedule (write the fill date on every can) so the reserve is always fresh.

Storage rules

Fire codes often limit how much gasoline you can keep at a home and where. Store fuel in approved containers in a detached shed or well-ventilated space away from ignition sources, never in the house or attached living space.

Plan the quantity

Work out gallons per day from your generator's runtime figures at your expected load, then multiply by the days you want to cover. Remember fuel stations need power to pump too.

Have a second fuel

A dual-fuel generator with both gasoline and propane on hand means one fuel running short or going stale doesn't stop you.

06Connecting to the house

There are three ways to get backup power to your loads. Only two of them are safe.

Extension cords

The simplest: run heavy-duty outdoor cords from the generator or power station straight to the appliances. Fine for a fridge, a freezer, and some lights. Can't reach hardwired loads like a well pump or furnace. Use cords rated for the load, and don't run them through doors or windows that then can't close.

Transfer switch or interlock

An electrician installs a power inlet outside and either a transfer switch or an interlock kit on the main panel. The house is disconnected from the grid before the generator feeds it, so you can run hardwired circuits safely. Required by electrical code for connecting a generator to house wiring.

Never backfeed

Never plug a generator into a wall outlet with a double-male cord. It sends power back out through your meter into the utility lines, where it can kill a lineworker or a neighbor, and it can burn up your wiring or generator when the grid comes back.

Whole-home batteries

Installed systems include their own disconnect and switch over automatically. They're tied into the panel by an installer, usually on a backed-up subpanel of critical circuits.

07Solar and recharging

Solar is what turns a battery from a one-time reserve into something that keeps going: silent, fuel-free power every day the sun is up. It only produces when the sun is up and much less in winter, under cloud, and in shade.

What to expect

A panel delivers roughly its rated watts times 3–5 "peak sun hours" a day, less losses. A 400 W panel is good for about 1–2 kWh on a decent day, and much less on a gray winter day.

Portable panels

Folding panels plug into most power stations. Enough to keep phones, radios, a router, and lights going; usually not enough to run a fridge and freezer indefinitely.

Charge controller

Panels feed a battery through a charge controller (MPPT is the efficient type). Power stations and whole-home systems have one built in; a 12 V DIY system needs its own.

Grid-tied panels go dark

Ordinary grid-tied solar shuts off during an outage for lineworker safety, even in full sun. To run from your panels in an outage, the system needs a battery or an inverter with a backup or islanding mode.

Mixing brands works if the panel's open-circuit voltage stays inside the station's solar input range and you have the right connector (most use MC4 or XT60, some Anderson or DC7909). Two panels wired in series double the voltage: check that against the input limit first.

08Heating and cooling

Heat and cooling are the biggest loads in most homes, and the hardest to run from a battery. A 1,500 W space heater empties a 1 kWh power station in about 40 minutes. The answer off-grid is to stop heating and cooling air with electricity where you can: store heat in mass, use the sun and evaporating water directly, and save electric power for fans, pumps, and the few devices that make heat efficiently.

How much a sand battery holds

Sand stores about 0.8 kJ per kg for every degree it's heated. A tonne of sand heated from 20 °C to 200 °C holds roughly 40 kWh of heat, about what a small home needs on a cold day. Getting it in takes a lot of solar: 1 kW of panels puts in roughly 3–5 kWh on a good winter day.

Sand battery reality check

The idea is proven: a commercial sand battery built by Polar Night Energy in Kankaanpää, Finland, has heated a district heating network since 2022, holding sand at around 500 °C. Home builds are another matter: they need high-temperature insulation, thermal cutoffs on the elements, a fire-safe location, and an electrician for the wiring. Running a heat pump straight from solar usually gets more heat per panel; a sand battery's advantage is storing summer-afternoon surplus as heat without a large electrical battery.

Building a water-over-brick cooler

Unglazed terracotta or porous brick, stacked so air passes through or along it; a trough or perforated pipe across the top; a catch basin and a small pump to recirculate. Put it in a breeze or in front of a fan, on the side air comes in from. Top up the water, clean the surface so mineral scale and algae don't block the pores, and keep standing water covered against mosquitoes.

Heat is a medical problem

Know the signs of heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache) and heat stroke (confusion, hot skin, collapse: an emergency). Cool the person fast with water and moving air. See Field Medical.

Never use a charcoal grill, camp stove, gas oven, or outdoor-only heater to heat a room. They produce carbon monoxide that builds up without warning. Keep battery-powered CO alarms working whenever anything burns inside.

09Safety

Carbon monoxide

Run generators outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from doors, windows, and vents. Never in a garage, even with the door open. Battery-powered CO alarms on every level, and get to fresh air at the first headache, dizziness, or nausea.

Refueling

Shut the generator off and let it cool before you refuel. Gasoline spilled on a hot engine can ignite.

Weather

Keep generators dry, under an open canopy or a purpose-made cover, and never touch one with wet hands or while standing in water.

Batteries

Use chargers made for the battery chemistry, fuse every DC circuit near the battery, keep batteries out of extreme heat, and give lead-acid batteries ventilation.

11Maintenance and testing

Monthly

Start the generator and run it under load for 20–30 minutes. Check that the power station and batteries are charged. Test the CO alarms.

Every few months

Rotate stored fuel. Top up stabilizer. Charge stored batteries to the level the manufacturer recommends for storage.

Yearly, or by hours

Change the generator's oil, air filter, and spark plug per the manual (the first oil change comes early, after break-in). Inspect cords, inlets, and the transfer equipment.

Test it for real

Once or twice a year, shut off the main breaker and run the house on backup for a few hours. You'll find the circuit you forgot and the load that trips the generator before an outage finds them for you.
