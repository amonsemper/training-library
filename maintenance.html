Cleaning and Maintenance

Keeping a gun reliable without wearing it out: making it safe first, a basic cleaning kit, how often to clean, where lubricant actually goes, parts that wear, checking your optic and mounts, function checks, and long-term storage for guns and ammunition.

Your firearm's manual is the authority. Intervals and lubrication points below are general guidance and vary by make and model. Anything beyond field-stripping (trigger work, headspace, barrel or bolt replacement) belongs with a qualified gunsmith.

01Make it safe first

No ammunition in the room

Unload, then take every round out of the room you're cleaning in. Most "it went off while I was cleaning it" stories start with ammunition on the bench.

Check twice, by eye and by touch

Remove the magazine first, lock the action open, then look into the chamber and magazine well and feel with a finger. Do it again before you start.

Lead and solvents

Clean in a ventilated space, wear nitrile gloves and eye protection, don't eat or drink at the bench, and wash your hands afterwards. Keep kids and pets away from solvents and fouling.

Muzzle discipline still applies

Point it in a safe direction the whole time, even unloaded, and especially while reassembling and function-checking.

03How often

Defensive and carry guns

Check and wipe down weekly; carry guns collect lint and sweat. Clean and re-lube after every range session so the gun you rely on is always ready.

After rain, salt air, or snow

Same day. Moisture starts rust within hours on carbon steel.

After corrosive surplus ammunition

Some older military surplus ammunition uses corrosive primers. Clean the bore, bolt, and gas system the same day with hot water or a water-based cleaner to dissolve the salts, dry thoroughly, then oil. Oil-based solvent alone doesn't remove them.

Don't over-clean

A properly lubricated modern gun runs thousands of rounds between deep cleanings. Excess scrubbing, especially from the muzzle end, can wear the crown and hurt accuracy.

04A basic cleaning

StepWhat to do
1Make it safe (section 01), then field-strip as the manual shows. Don't go further than the manual's field strip.
2Bore: a wet patch, let the solvent sit a few minutes, brush a few passes, then dry patches. Work from the chamber end when the design allows.
3Bolt, slide, and action: brush carbon off the bolt face, extractor, and feed ramp; wipe the slide rails and frame.
4Wipe everything dry, then lubricate only where section 05 says.
5Reassemble and do a function check (section 08).
6Wipe the outside with a lightly oiled cloth to remove fingerprints, which cause rust on blued steel.

05Where lubricant goes

The rule: lubricate where metal slides on metal, which you can see from the shiny wear marks. Everywhere else gets a light protective film at most. Too much oil collects dirt and carbon.

PlatformLubricateKeep dry
AR-15Generously on the bolt carrier rails, the cam pin, and the bolt and its gas rings; a light film on the charging handle. ARs run best wet.Firing pin channel, magazines
Striker-fired pistol (Glock-type)A drop each on the slide rails, the outside of the barrel and barrel hood, and where the trigger bar meets the connector.Firing pin channel (oil there gums up and causes light strikes), magazines
Hammer-fired pistol (1911, CZ, etc.)Slide and frame rails, barrel bushing or barrel hood, the disconnector and hammer contact points.Magazines, grips
Pump shotgunAction bars, the bolt slide, and the magazine tube where the forend rides.Chamber, shells
Bolt-action rifleBolt lugs, lug recesses, and the cocking cam, with a little grease on the lugs.Chamber, trigger group (unless the manual says otherwise)

Never oil ammunition or let solvent touch primers: it can deactivate them.

06Parts that wear

Springs and small parts wear long before barrels and frames. Many makers publish round-count intervals; your manual's number wins. Keep a log of round counts and replace parts before they fail, not after.

Recoil springs (pistols)

The most common wear item. A weak spring causes failures to feed and battering. Many makers suggest replacing every few thousand rounds.

Extractors and their springs

Failures to extract, or brass ejecting erratically, often point here. Inexpensive to replace.

Magazine springs and followers

Replace when a magazine starts causing feeding problems. Number your magazines so you can track the bad one.

AR bolts and gas rings

Inspect the bolt lugs for cracks and the cam pin hole for wear at cleanings; gas rings wear gradually.

07Optics, lights, and mounts

Batteries on a calendar

Replace red dot and light batteries on a schedule (an annual date is easy to remember), not when they die. Keep a spare in the grip or on the sling.

Torque and witness marks

Mount screws to the maker's torque spec with a torque driver, then paint a thin line across each screw head and the mount. If the line breaks, the screw moved.

Clean lenses gently

Blow off grit first, then a lens pen or microfiber. Rubbing sand across the glass scratches the coatings.

Clean the light

Carbon from shooting builds up on a weapon light's lens and cuts its output. Wipe it at every cleaning.

08Function checks

After reassembly, with the gun confirmed empty and pointed in a safe direction, check that every control works as designed. Example for an AR-15:

StepExpected result
Charge the rifle, put it on safe, press the triggerThe hammer does not fall
Switch to fire, press the trigger and hold itThe hammer falls
Still holding the trigger, charge the rifle again, then release the trigger slowlyA click as the hammer resets onto the trigger
Press the trigger againThe hammer falls

Every platform has its own function check in its manual. If any step fails, don't load it: take it to a gunsmith.

09Storage

Control humidity

Aim for about 30–50% relative humidity in a safe. A rechargeable desiccant canister or a safe dehumidifier rod plus a cheap hygrometer tells you if it's working.

Light oil film

Wipe metal surfaces with a thin coat of protectant before long-term storage. Silicone-treated gun socks help; foam-lined cases hold moisture against steel and shouldn't be used for long-term storage.

Ammunition

Cool and dry, in its original boxes or sealed ammo cans with a desiccant pack, away from solvents and oils. Avoid big temperature swings.

Secure it

Locked and out of reach of children and unauthorized people. Many states have safe-storage laws; know yours.
