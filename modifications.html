Common Weapon Modifications

The modifications that change how a gun actually works in use: short barrels, suppressors, weapon lights, aiming lasers (visible and infrared), and optics. For each one, what it does, what it costs you, and how it behaves on the AR-15, the pump shotgun, the handgun, and the bolt rifle.

The legal section describes U.S. federal law as of October 2026, which changed significantly this year and is still in litigation. State and local law is separate and often stricter. This is not legal advice: confirm the current rules with ATF, your state, and an attorney before you buy, build, or travel.

01The legal picture

ItemFederal definition
Short-barreled rifle (SBR)A rifle with a barrel under 16 inches, or an overall length under 26 inches
Short-barreled shotgun (SBS)A shotgun with a barrel under 18 inches, or an overall length under 26 inches
Silencer (suppressor)Any device for silencing, muffling, or diminishing the report of a portable firearm, including its parts

What changed in 2026

  • January 1: the federal tax on making or transferring an SBR, SBS, suppressor, or "any other weapon" dropped from $200 to $0. Machine guns and destructive devices still carry the $200 tax.
  • August 5: a federal court in Texas (Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF) held that, with no tax left, the registration and approval requirements for those items were no longer supported by Congress's taxing power. The order covered the plaintiffs.
  • October 9: ATF announced it would no longer require approval or registration for SBRs, SBSs, suppressors, and AOWs from anyone, as a matter of enforcement discretion.

What didn't change

  • The National Firearms Act is still law. The October policy is enforcement discretion and can be reversed, and the case is still being litigated.
  • State and local law. Several states and D.C. ban civilian suppressors, SBRs, or both, and many allow them only when federally registered.
  • Background checks at dealers, prohibited-person rules, and dealer recordkeeping under the Gun Control Act.
  • ATF still asks owners to file Form 5320.20 before moving an SBR or SBS across state lines.
  • Machine guns and destructive devices remain fully regulated.

Before you buy or build: check your state's law, check ATF's current guidance, and keep records of how and when you acquired the item. If the federal policy reverses, you will want to be able to register, and a mistake about state law can be a felony.

Pistol stabilizing braces: ATF's 2023 rule treating many braced pistols as SBRs was vacated by a federal court in 2024. Braced AR pistols are again sold as pistols; a brace used as a stock is a practical, not a legal, distinction that may still matter under state law.

02Short barrels (SBR / SBS)

A shorter barrel makes the gun handier in vehicles, hallways, and doorways, and lighter at the muzzle so it swings and stops faster. In exchange, you lose velocity, gain blast and flash, and sometimes reliability.

AR-15

The most common SBR. Under about 12 inches, many loads lose the velocity needed to expand or fragment reliably past 50–100 yards. Shorter gas systems run harder; adjustable gas blocks and heavier buffers help. A 300 Blackout upper keeps more of its performance in a short barrel.

Garand Thumb: What is the best caliber for your SBR?2:12

Pump shotgun

A 14-inch barrel is the common SBS length. Buckshot patterns change little with barrel length, but magazine capacity drops with the shorter tube, and the muzzle is easier to short-stroke when the gun is light. A Mossberg 590 with a 14-inch barrel holds about 5 shells in the tube, against 8 for a 20-inch 590A1.

Handgun

Handguns are already short. Adding a vertical foregrip to a pistol makes it an "any other weapon" (AOW); adding a stock makes it an SBR. A pistol-caliber carbine with a short barrel gains little velocity but is much easier to shoot accurately than a pistol.

Bolt rifle

A 16–18 inch bolt gun is common and legal without any registration. Shorter is rarely worth it: magnum and long-range cartridges lose much of their performance.

03Suppressors

A suppressor traps and slows the expanding powder gas at the muzzle. It reduces the report by roughly 20–35 decibels, cuts most of the flash, and lowers recoil. It does not make a gun silent: a suppressed 5.56 rifle is still about as loud as a jackhammer, and any supersonic bullet still makes a sharp crack along its path. Exposure limits for impulse noise are usually set at 140 dB, and many suppressed rifles only get down to that level, so keep wearing ear protection when you can.

Mounting

Direct-thread mounts are lighter and simpler. Quick-detach mounts let one can move between guns but add length and cost. Match the thread pitch (1/2×28 for most 5.56 and 9mm, 5/8×24 for most .30 caliber) and caliber rating, and check alignment before shooting to avoid a baffle strike.

Brass Facts: Buying a suppressor: mounting and interfaces13:25

Heat

A suppressor can reach several hundred degrees after a few magazines. Burns and melted gear are common. Use a cover, and don't lean the gun against anything with the can still hot.

04Weapon lights

A light mounted on a defensive gun is for one job: identifying what you're about to shoot at. Use a handheld light for searching and navigating. A weapon light on in your hand is a muzzle pointed at everything it lights.

Dirty Civilian: Why you need a weapon-mounted light1:20

Lumens and candela

Lumens measure total output; candela measures the intensity of the beam's hotspot, which decides how far it reaches and how well it punches through shadows and smoke. A handgun needs about 500–1,000 lumens; a rifle used outdoors benefits from 10,000+ candela more than from more lumens.

Brass Facts: Rifle light primer: lumens, candela, mounting1:47

Activation

Momentary on is the default: light on when you need it, off when you move. A pressure switch or tail cap must be reachable without changing your grip. Practice so the light comes on before you need it, not after.

05Visible lasers

A red or green aiming laser shows where the bullet will land at the distance it's zeroed. It helps when you can't get your eye behind the sights: around cover, from awkward positions, wearing a gas mask, or shooting one-handed. It doesn't replace sights. Daylight washes it out, and chasing the dot under stress can make the shooter slower.

  • Zero it for the distance you'll use it, usually 10–25 yards for a handgun or 25–50 yards for a long gun. The laser sits below or beside the bore, so the dot and point of impact only agree at the zero distance.
  • Green is easier to see in daylight; red uses less battery.
  • On a handgun, grip- or frame-activated lasers come on as you grip the gun. On long guns they're usually combined with the light in a single unit.
  • Never point a laser at an aircraft. It's a federal crime (18 U.S.C. § 39A), and even a few milliwatts can blind a pilot.

06Infrared lasers and illuminators

Infrared aiming lasers and illuminators work only with night vision devices; the beam is invisible to the naked eye. Under night vision, the shooter can aim with the IR laser while the device is on their head, without putting a rifle optic to their eye, and the IR illuminator lights the area for the night vision without visible light.

Where it works

Mainly the AR-15 and other carbines, used with a helmet-mounted night vision device. Some handgun units exist; on shotguns and bolt rifles it's rare. Civilian units are lower power than military versions.

07Optics

An optic puts the aiming point on the same focal plane as the target, so you can look at the threat instead of focusing on a front sight. Which kind is right depends on distance, light, and how much weight and battery dependence you'll accept.

Dot size

A 2 MOA dot covers about 2 inches at 100 yards and allows precise shots on a rifle. Larger dots (3–6.5 MOA) are faster to find on a pistol. A circle-dot reticle gives a big reference for speed and a small dot for precision.

Mount height and co-witness

Absolute co-witness (about 1.41" over an AR's rail) puts the iron sights in the center of the window; lower 1/3 (about 1.54") puts them in the bottom of it. Taller 1.93" mounts suit a heads-up posture, helmets, and night vision, but add mechanical offset: the bullet hits farther below the dot up close.

Garand Thumb: Fighting rifle setup: optic height7:25

Eye relief and eye box

Dots and holographics have unlimited eye relief. Prisms (about 1.5–2.5") and LPVOs (about 3.5–4") need your eye in one spot; if it isn't, the picture blacks out at the edges. Mount them so the full picture appears with your head in its natural place on the stock.

Garand Thumb: Fighting rifle setup: eye relief with an LPVO8:52

First or second focal plane

A first-focal-plane reticle grows and shrinks with the magnification, so its holds are correct at every power. A second-focal-plane reticle stays the same size, which keeps it bold at 1×, but its holds are correct at only one power, usually the highest.

Brass Facts: LPVO philosophy of use: FFP vs. SFP18:48

Match the units

Use a reticle and turrets in the same unit: a mil reticle with 0.1-mil clicks, or an MOA reticle with 1/4-MOA clicks. Mixing them means converting every correction under pressure. See the Ballistics Calculator for holds in both.

Mounting and batteries

Torque to the maker's numbers (often 15–25 in-lb for ring screws and 35–65 in-lb for a rail clamp), level the reticle, and mark screws with a paint pen so you can see if they move. Change batteries on a fixed date each year, carry a spare, and keep iron sights on a defensive gun.

Garand Thumb: Mounting an optic in scope rings0:00

AR-15

A red dot or holographic for most defensive use, an LPVO when you need distance, and a prism for a fixed, battery-proof setup. Set the mount height so your head stays upright.

Pump shotgun

A small red dot on the receiver helps most with slugs past 50 yards. Ghost-ring irons are the durable alternative. Magnification adds little.

Handgun

A slide-mounted dot on a cut slide or adapter plate, matched to the slide's footprint (RMR, RMSc, ACRO, DeltaPoint). Taller "suppressor-height" irons let you see them through the window.

Garand Thumb: Red dots on pistols: pros and cons0:41Garand Thumb: Pistol optics compared: windows, batteries, mounting2:12

Bolt rifle

A variable scope with rings sized to the tube (30 or 34 mm). For long range, a 20 MOA canted base leaves more elevation in the turret. See the Bolt Rifle page for setup.
