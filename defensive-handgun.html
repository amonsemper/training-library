Defensive Handgun Shooting

Carrying and using a handgun for self-defense: the legal framework, awareness, carry gear, the reactionary gap, drawing from concealment, moving off the line, close-contact shooting, multiple threats, low light, and what to do afterward.

Built on common defensive handgun curricula, the Tueller drill, and general U.S. self-defense law, with Missouri's statute linked as an example. This is training information, not legal advice. Laws differ by state; learn yours from a qualified attorney or instructor. Right-handed shooter assumed.

01Legal framework

In most U.S. jurisdictions, deadly force is justified only when you reasonably believe it's immediately necessary to prevent death or serious bodily harm to yourself or another innocent person. "Reasonably" is judged by what an ordinary, prudent person would believe in your position, knowing what you knew.

Ability

The attacker has the means to kill or seriously injure: a weapon, or a large disparity of force (size, numbers, skill).

Opportunity

They can use that ability now: within range of the weapon or close enough to reach you.

Jeopardy

Their actions show intent: words plus actions that would make a reasonable person believe the attack is coming.

Preclusion / avoidance

Some states require retreat if you can do so in complete safety; many don't ("stand your ground"). Even where not required, avoiding the fight is always the best outcome.

Example: Missouri

Missouri's § 563.031 allows deadly force when you reasonably believe it's necessary to protect against death, serious physical injury, or a forcible felony. It removes the duty to retreat from your dwelling, residence, vehicle, private property you own or lease, and any place you have a right to be. It also covers someone who unlawfully enters, or tries to enter, your dwelling, residence, or vehicle. Read the statute itself and get advice from a Missouri attorney; the details and exceptions matter.

  • Force must stop when the threat stops. Shots fired after the threat has ended are judged separately.
  • Being the initial aggressor generally removes the self-defense justification.
  • Brandishing or displaying a firearm when deadly force isn't justified can itself be a crime.

02Awareness and decision

Pre-attack indicators

  • Someone moving to intercept your path or closing distance for no reason
  • Hands hidden, or repeatedly touching the waistband
  • Looking around for witnesses, or coordinating glances with others
  • "Target glancing": looking at you, then away, then back
  • Sudden change in behavior, or a pretext question to get close ("got the time?")

Options before the gun

  • Avoid: cross the street, change direction, don't enter.
  • Escape: leave as soon as something feels wrong.
  • Verbal: "Stop! Stay back!" with a hand up. It establishes you as the one who tried to avoid the fight.
  • Distance and barriers: put a car or counter between you.

Holster requirements

  • Fully covers the trigger guard
  • Rigid (Kydex or reinforced leather) so the mouth stays open for one-handed holstering
  • Holds the gun securely through running, jumping, and turning upside down
  • Attaches firmly so it comes out with the belt, not with the gun

Everything else

  • A stiff gun belt; regular belts sag and shift.
  • Spare magazine: more for fixing malfunctions than for capacity.
  • A small handheld light.
  • A tourniquet and basic trauma kit. You're more likely to need it for someone else than the gun.

04The reactionary gap

In 1983 Sgt. Dennis Tueller found that an average adult could cover 21 feet in about 1.5 seconds, roughly the time it took officers to draw and fire from a duty holster. The lesson isn't that 21 feet is a magic number. It's that a person with a contact weapon can be on you before you can shoot, unless you already have distance, a head start, or are moving.

21 feet, two ways
  • Reaction time alone (seeing, deciding, starting to move) is about 0.25–0.5 s for a prepared person, longer if surprised.
  • A draw from concealment to a first hit is typically 1.5–2.0 s for a trained shooter.
  • Moving off the attacker's line while drawing makes them change direction, which buys time and can avoid the first contact.

05Drawing from concealment

Open-front garment: sweep, grip, draw
GarmentClearing technique
Open-front (jacket, open shirt)Strong hand sweeps the garment back, past the holster, and drives down onto the grip in one motion. Weight in the pocket helps it stay back.
Closed-front (T-shirt, sweater), strong sideSupport hand grabs the shirt and pulls it up high toward the chest; strong hand gets the grip.
Closed-front, appendixOne or both hands pull the shirt up hard; strong hand takes the grip. A support-hand "pec pull" lifts the shirt to the collarbone.

Practice the draw with the clothes you actually wear, in dry practice first. Re-holster slowly, sweeping the garment out of the way and looking the gun into the holster.

06Close-contact shooting

When an attacker is at arm's length, extending the gun puts it within their reach. Shooting from a retention position, gun close to the body, keeps it away from their hands.

  • Use the support hand to strike, push, or shield, then shoot from retention.
  • Step back or to the side to create space as you shoot, then extend to a full grip.
  • Pressing the muzzle against an attacker can push some semi-autos out of battery; keep a little space.

07Moving off the line

Why move

An attacker aims (or charges) at where you are. Moving laterally or diagonally makes them adjust, which costs them time. Moving toward cover also improves what happens next.

How

  • First step is big and explosive, to the side and slightly forward or back.
  • Draw while moving; shoot when the gun is up and the shot is available. Stopping to shoot is often more accurate than shooting on the move.
  • Moving straight backward is slow and risks tripping. Go diagonal.

08Assess and scan

After shooting, the danger isn't automatically over. The attacker may still be a threat, have a partner, or there may be bystanders in your line of fire. Assessment is deliberate.

After the shots
  1. Assess the threat over the sights, then just below them so you can see hands and weapon. Is the threat still able and trying to hurt you?
  2. Breathe and break tunnel vision. Stress narrows your field of view.
  3. Scan left, right, and behind, turning your head and body. Look for other threats, a partner, and bystanders.
  4. Move to cover or away from the scene if it's safe to do so.
  5. Top off or reload if you can, then consider holstering only when the situation is truly over.

09Multiple threats

Engage the most dangerous threat first, which is usually the nearest one, or the one with a gun pointed at you. Eyes move to the next target first, and the gun follows the eyes.

Eyes lead, gun follows
  • Move so the threats line up or so one blocks the others.
  • Don't overswing: stop the gun on the next target, settle, then press.
  • After the last threat, come back across all of them to assess. The first one may still be active.

10One-handed shooting

Strong hand only

  • Grip as high and tight as possible; lock the wrist.
  • Rotate the gun slightly inward (about 15–30°) to align with the natural line of the arm.
  • Lean forward; the arm recovers from recoil slower without the support hand.

Support hand only

  • Same grip principles with the other hand.
  • Close the strong-side eye if you can't use the support-side eye with the sights.
  • Practice support-hand draws (reaching across) and transfers from the strong hand in dry practice.
  • Practice one-handed malfunction clearances: rack the slide on a belt, holster edge, or heel.

Light, identify, act or turn it off, move. In a dark house, a burst of light can disorient an intruder and lets you see hands. Always identify; the person in your hallway at night may be a family member.

12After an incident

Immediately

  1. Stay alert; scan; get to a safe position.
  2. Holster when the threat is over, or keep the gun at low ready if not.
  3. Call 911.
  4. Holster or put the gun down before police arrive. Empty hands visible. Follow every command, even if it's uncomfortable.
  5. Render aid if it's safe and you're trained.

What to say to 911

"My name is ___. I'm at ___. I was attacked and had to defend myself. Someone is injured; send police and an ambulance. I'm wearing ___. I'm armed; my gun is ___ (holstered / on the ground)."

When police arrive

  • Identify yourself as the person who called and was attacked.
  • Point out evidence (the attacker's weapon, shell casings) and witnesses before they leave.
  • Say you'll sign a complaint and will cooperate fully after speaking with your attorney.
  • Don't give a detailed statement while adrenaline is high. Memory, distance, and time estimates are distorted after a violent encounter.
  • Get medical attention; adrenaline masks injuries.
