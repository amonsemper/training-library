Fieldcraft

How small teams move and stay unseen outside of buildings: finding your way with a map and compass, choosing a hide site, moving without leaving a trail, managing what you give off in visible light, infrared, and thermal, living under drones, watching from an observation post, getting out of a city, moving a casualty, and dividing the work across a team.

Fieldcraft is learned outdoors, not on a page. Practice each skill on familiar ground, in daylight first, before you need it somewhere unfamiliar at night.

01Land navigation

GPS fails: batteries die, signals are jammed or spoofed, and a glowing screen gives you away at night. A paper map and a compass don't. The aim is to always know roughly where you are, so a check confirms your position instead of finding it from scratch.

The map

Learn the marginal information first: scale, contour interval, grid zone, and declination diagram. Military maps use MGRS grids; a six-digit grid locates you to 100 meters, an eight-digit grid to 10. Read grids right, then up. Keep the map in a waterproof case, folded to your area.

Garand Thumb: Map and compass: map components and grids2:48

The compass

A lensatic or baseplate compass gives a magnetic azimuth. The map uses grid north, so convert with the declination from the map's diagram (the old mnemonic: "east is least, west is best" when converting grid to magnetic). Hold it away from steel, rifles, and radios.

Dirty Civilian: Land nav basics: the compass1:56

Terrain association

Orient the map so its features line up with the ground, then match hills, saddles, draws, ridges, and streams to the contour lines. Contour lines close together mean steep ground; a V pointing uphill is a draw or stream.

Garand Thumb: Map and compass: terrain association28:21

Distance

Keep a pace count: the number of steps you take per 100 meters on flat ground, usually 60–70 for an adult. It rises on slopes, in brush, and under load. Ranger beads let you count without thinking about it.

02Leaving the area and hide sites

When a plan falls apart, the first move is to break contact and put distance and terrain between you and whatever went wrong. The first stop is usually short and hasty; the deliberate hide comes later, once you know you weren't followed.

Get out first

Leave by a route that puts terrain between you and the threat as quickly as possible. Don't stop at the first comfortable spot. Agree on a rally point and a time limit in advance so a separated team can find each other.

Garand Thumb: Evading a professional military: getting out4:14

Pick the unlikely spot

Searchers check obvious hiding places first: the thickest cover, the only tree line, buildings. A small, unremarkable patch of low cover away from trails, with a clear view of approaches and a back door, is better than the best-looking thicket.

Garand Thumb: Evading as a two-man team: the first hide site8:14

Approach with a buttonhook

Walk past the hide site, then loop back to it from downwind so anyone following your trail walks past you first. Enter the last few meters slowly and leave no sign at the entrance.

Living in it

Low profile, minimal movement, no cooking smell or light. One person on watch at all times. Keep gear packed so you can leave in seconds, and know your escape route and the next rally point before you settle in.

03Moving without a trail

Trackers read sign: anything you disturb. Ground sign is footprints, scuffs, overturned stones, and broken sticks; top sign is bent or broken vegetation above the ankle, scraped bark, and disturbed dew or spider webs. You can't leave nothing, but you can leave less, leave it where it's hard to see, and make following it slow.

Garand Thumb: Tracking and countertracking32:48

Reduce sign

  • Step on rock, roots, leaf litter, and hard ground; avoid mud, sand, snow, and dew-wet grass.
  • Move around vegetation rather than through it, and don't break branches.
  • Walk in each other's footprints in single file to hide the number of people.
  • Pack out everything, and bury nothing that animals can dig up.
Garand Thumb: Evading as a two-man team: movement17:05

Slow the tracker

  • Cross hard surfaces, streams, and roads at angles that don't line up with your direction of travel.
  • Change direction often, and after a few changes make a large one.
  • Avoid trails and the easy, obvious line through terrain that anyone would take.
  • Move at night or in rain, which hide sign and slow searchers.

04Signature: visible, IR, thermal

Anything that makes you stand out from the background gives you away: your shape, shine, shadow, silhouette, spacing, movement, sound, and electronic signals. Modern sensors add two more spectrums. Night vision sees near-infrared light, including IR lasers and illuminators, and thermal sees heat. Each needs its own countermeasure.

Dirty Civilian: Signature reduction 1012:31
SpectrumWhat gives you awayWhat helps
VisibleRegular outlines (head, shoulders, rifle), shine from skin, glass, and metal, skylining on ridges, movement, spacing in a straight lineBreak up outlines with local vegetation, cover skin and glass, move below crests, stay in shadow, move slowly and only when needed
Near-infrared (night vision)IR lasers and illuminators, some fabrics and camo patterns that glow under NV, screens, indicator LEDsUse IR only when necessary and briefly, IR-treated clothing, cover LEDs and screens, assume the other side has night vision too
ThermalBody heat (especially head, face, and hands), warm engines and exhaust, recently fired barrels, disturbed ground, campfiresMass between you and the sensor (earth, rock, thick logs, vehicles), insulating layers and purpose-built thermal blankets, staying still, moving when the ground is close to body temperature
ElectronicPhones, radios, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, GPS devices that transmit, smart watchesTurn off or leave behind what you don't need, keep transmissions short and scheduled

Thermal is not magic

Thermal sensors see surface temperature, not through walls, glass, dense foliage, or solid objects. Rain, fog, and ground that has warmed to near body temperature in the afternoon reduce contrast. A thermal blanket or poncho hides a still person, but a warm patch of ground stays after you leave.

Dirty Civilian: Defeating thermal optics: myth busting7:00

Movement beats camouflage

Every sensor, human eyes included, picks up movement first. A well-camouflaged person who moves is easier to spot than a poorly camouflaged one who stays still. Move when the observer is least likely to be looking, and freeze when you think you've been seen.

05Drones

Small drones put an observer overhead, often with a thermal camera. Some carry munitions. A drone sees what a person on a hill sees, from directly above, and can follow you. Overhead cover now matters as much as cover from the front.

Garand Thumb: Evading a thermal drone: understanding the threat3:03Garand Thumb: Evading a professional military: drones and thermals (2026)0:00

Detection

Many small drones are heard before they're seen: a high-pitched buzz that changes as they maneuver. Assign someone to listen and look up. Commercial drones hover and observe; FPV drones fly fast and low toward a target.

Hiding from above

Tree canopy, building overhangs, and vehicles block the view. Stay under them, freeze when a drone is close, and don't look up with an uncovered face. In thermal, mass overhead (a thick tree trunk, a building, a vehicle) beats thin cover.

Garand Thumb: Evading a thermal drone: thermal camouflage16:18

Moving under drones

Move in short bounds between overhead cover, spread out so one camera frame doesn't capture everyone, and change direction once out of sight, since an operator watching you will predict where you're heading. A sharp hook after breaking line of sight can lose a following drone.

Garand Thumb: Evading a thermal drone: the J-hook22:27

Engaging them

Shooting a drone is hard: it's small, moving, and often closer or farther than it looks. A shotgun with birdshot is more effective than a rifle at short range. Laws on interfering with aircraft apply; know them before you act outside a war zone.

Brass Facts: Drone defeat for civilians: what works13:24

06Recon and observation posts

Recon answers specific questions without being seen. The CQB Primer covers recon fundamentals, the SALUTE report, and actions at the objective; this section covers watching from a fixed position.

Brass Facts: Civilian small-team reconnaissance0:10Dirty Civilian: Civilian recon: a case for recon1:30

Choosing an OP

It needs a view of the objective, a covered and concealed route in and out, cover from fire, and concealment from the likely directions of observation, including from above. Avoid the most obvious vantage point, which is where the other side will look first.

Dirty Civilian: Observation posts and hasty hide sites11:15

Running it

Two people minimum: one observes and records, one provides security and rests. Rotate before the observer's attention fades, usually every 20–30 minutes. Log times, counts, descriptions, and directions exactly as seen.

Optics

Binoculars or a spotting scope for detail, a monocular for quick checks, and night vision or thermal after dark. Shade the lenses with a hood or mesh, and keep glass back from the edge of the hide so it doesn't reflect.

Brass Facts: Pocket recon: a monocular0:00

Reporting

Report what you saw, not what you think it means, using a set format such as SALUTE: size, activity, location, unit or uniform, time, and equipment. Agree in advance when to report and what triggers an immediate report.

Dirty Civilian: Civilian recon: the SALUTE report6:30

07Urban movement

In a city, the threat to a small group moving through it is being noticed. The terrain is full of people, cameras, and lines of sight from above. The goal is to look like you belong and to avoid places where you'd stand out.

Garand Thumb: Escaping the city: movement12:09

Blend in

Dress and carry yourself like the people around you. Visible kit, matching outfits, and a group moving in formation attract attention. Split a group into pairs that move separately and link up at planned points.

Garand Thumb: Urban survival: camouflage in the city4:30

Routes

Avoid chokepoints such as bridges, tunnels, and main roads that can be closed or watched. Know several routes out, and use alleys, parks, rail lines, and drainage channels where they help. Move at the times when the streets are busy enough to hide in, or empty enough to see who's watching.

Brass Facts: Civilian urban recce6:38

Stopping

Hasty hides in a city are unoccupied buildings, garages, and roofs, but each is a box with limited exits. Pick spots with two ways out and a view of the approach, and don't stay long.

Garand Thumb: Escaping the city: the hide site22:48

Urban is not CQB

Most of a city fight happens across streets, from windows and rooftops, at 50–300 meters, not inside rooms. A rifle with some magnification and a team that can observe high and low matter as much as room-clearing skills.

Brass Facts: Urban is not CQB5:29

08Moving a casualty

A casualty changes everything: the team's speed, its route, and how many people are free to fight. Treat life threats first (massive bleeding, airway, breathing), then plan the move. Carrying a person is exhausting, so rotate carriers often.

09Team roles

A small team works when each person has a primary job and a backup for someone else's. Pick roles from what people are already good at, then cross-train so the team survives losing anyone.
