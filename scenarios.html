Tactics · Incident studies

Scenario-Based Engagement Breakdowns

These incident studies are intended as discussion pieces rather than doctrine. The focus is on how small groups respond when plans change, information becomes incomplete, personnel are lost, and the environment begins limiting the options that seemed available at the start.

01Armed Offenders Conducting an Urban Fighting Withdrawal

A heavily armed group attempting to leave an urban objective encountered a rapidly developing local police response. Their original objective was already complete, so from that point forward their primary concern was escape.

The offenders possessed rifles, body armor, vehicles, and enough coordination to maintain movement during the early portion of the response. Their immediate advantage was firepower and initiative. Law enforcement had a different advantage: the response could continue to grow. The longer the incident lasted, the more officers could arrive, the more information could be shared, and the fewer uncontested routes would remain available.

Early in the engagement, the offenders were repeatedly able to create temporary freedom of movement. They engaged responding officers, moved to another position, and continued trying to leave the area. This worked for a time, but each movement addressed only the immediate problem. It did not improve their overall situation.

They remained in a populated urban environment where vehicles restricted movement and buildings and intersections repeatedly broke visual contact between members of the group. At the same time, officers were entering the area from additional directions. As the response expanded, the offenders gradually became separated from one another.

That fragmentation became more significant than any individual exchange of gunfire. While the offenders remained together, they could communicate directly, observe the same problems, and react as a group. Once separated, each individual was working with less information about where the others were, what condition they were in, and which routes were still available.

Casualties made that problem worse. A wounded or lost member did not simply reduce the group by one person; it changed how the remaining members moved and what they were capable of doing together. Individual offenders continued to act aggressively and, in several instances, effectively, but the group as a whole was becoming less coordinated.

This is where the distinction between performing well in an individual confrontation and controlling the larger incident becomes important. The offenders were still able to gain another intersection, reach another vehicle, force officers to reposition, or temporarily open a route. Those were meaningful short-term successes, but none of them restored the initiative they had at the beginning.

As the incident continued, time increasingly favored law enforcement. Additional officers were arriving while the offenders were losing people, routes, mobility, and contact with one another. Eventually the original withdrawal stopped functioning as a coordinated plan and became a series of individual attempts to leave the area.

The useful point is that the group did not suddenly collapse. Its ability to function deteriorated progressively as delays, casualties, separation, and the expanding police response accumulated. By the time the offenders were operating largely as individuals, they were dealing with a very different situation from the one they had encountered at the start.

02Security Convoy Contact at a Congested Border Checkpoint

A protected vehicle convoy transporting a detainee became immobilized in heavy traffic while approaching an international border checkpoint. Civilian vehicles surrounded the convoy, traffic prevented meaningful maneuver, and adjacent vehicles restricted visibility. If a threat developed, there was very little room to create distance or change the formation of the convoy.

Because mobility was limited, observation became increasingly important. Before any overt attack occurred, members of the security element began noticing behavior that differed from the surrounding traffic. Certain vehicles drew additional attention, some occupants appeared unusually interested in the convoy, and people began opening doors and moving in ways that caused the security personnel to pay closer attention.

At that point, the situation was still uncertain. Nobody had yet done anything that clearly established hostile intent. That period of uncertainty is probably the most useful part of the incident because the security personnel had to prepare for a possible confrontation without treating everyone around them as a threat.

There was a noticeable difference in how quickly various people recognized that the situation might be changing. Some members of the convoy were still trying to determine whether anything significant was actually occurring, while others had already seen enough indicators to increase their level of readiness.

Once weapons were presented and the threat became clear, the confrontation developed and ended very quickly. The physical distances were short, the vehicles provided little maneuver space, and the security personnel were already paying attention to the people who became involved.

What makes the incident particularly useful for discussion is how little of it consisted of actual gunfire. Most of the significant decision-making occurred beforehand, while the convoy was stationary and the situation remained ambiguous. Personnel were observing behavior, comparing what they were seeing against the surrounding environment, communicating their concerns, and preparing themselves without unnecessarily escalating the situation.

After the confrontation ended, the surrounding environment returned almost immediately to normal activity. Traffic remained backed up, civilians were still present, and the convoy continued toward the checkpoint. That abrupt transition reinforces how quickly a violent encounter can develop inside an otherwise routine environment.

The convoy had very few options for physical movement, so its greatest advantage was recognizing that the situation was changing before the threat became completely obvious. The incident is therefore less about the brief exchange itself and more about the period immediately before it, when personnel were attempting to distinguish unusual behavior from an actual developing threat.

03Isolated Position Under Repeated Assault

A lightly equipped security element occupied an isolated compound in an increasingly hostile area with limited prospects for immediate reinforcement. The location was exposed, support was distant, resupply was uncertain, and the surrounding terrain allowed an attacking force to approach from several directions.

Before the first major attack occurred, the local commander recognized that the position needed improvement and directed personnel to prepare it accordingly. That decision did not eliminate the problems associated with the location, but it gave the defenders more options once the attacks began.

The attacking force possessed a substantial numerical advantage and made repeated attempts to overwhelm the position. The defenders were rarely in a position to control the larger fight. Instead, they dealt with whichever part of the perimeter was under the most pressure at a given moment, shifting personnel and attention as conditions changed.

That approach worked for a considerable period of time, but it came at a cost. Every successful defense consumed ammunition, water, medical resources, energy, and time. Those losses were difficult or impossible to replace because the position remained isolated.

This created a situation where the defenders could perform well in the immediate engagement while their overall condition continued to deteriorate. Stopping another assault was important, but it did not necessarily leave them in a better position afterward. In some cases, it simply meant they were still holding the same ground with fewer resources available for the next attack.

As the engagement continued, the central question gradually changed. It was no longer just whether the defenders could repel another assault. It became whether they could continue doing so with the supplies and support that remained.

That distinction is important because a position does not have to be physically overrun before it becomes unsustainable. Personnel may still be organized, communicating, and capable of responding effectively while the range of available options is steadily narrowing.

The useful discussion point is the difference between solving the immediate tactical problem and improving the overall situation. The defenders were very good at the first. The isolation of the position made the second much harder.

04Fragmented Ground Force Responding to Aircraft Losses in an Urban Area

A combined assault and security force entered a dense urban area expecting to conduct a relatively short operation. The original objective was achieved, but the situation changed almost immediately when an aircraft went down inside the city.

Ground elements that had been positioned around the original objective were redirected toward the crash location. Vehicle columns attempted to reach the same area through narrow streets while hostile personnel established roadblocks and firing positions along the likely routes.

The first aircraft loss already required the force to reorganize around a problem that had not existed in the original plan. When a second aircraft went down at another location, the operation became even more fragmented.

At that point, there was no longer one clear problem. Personnel at the original objective still required security. The first crash site needed to be reached and protected. The second crash created another isolated group requiring assistance. Casualties needed treatment and evacuation, and vehicle elements were still trying to move through streets that were becoming increasingly difficult to navigate.

Every new problem consumed resources that had already been assigned somewhere else. A casualty did not only remove one person from the fight. Someone had to treat that person, someone might have to move him, and additional personnel might be required to provide security while that happened.

The same effect occurred with the downed aircraft. The aircraft itself was only part of the problem. The crew, the crash site, the recovery effort, and the personnel trying to reach it all became additional responsibilities.

Urban terrain amplified those difficulties. Friendly elements could be physically close to each other while still being unable to make contact. A few city blocks could contain barricades, hostile fire, damaged vehicles, unfamiliar streets, crowds, and casualties. On a map, the distance might appear minor. On the ground, it could be enough to keep two friendly groups effectively separated.

Communication also became increasingly difficult because events were developing faster than information could be collected and passed. A report could be accurate when transmitted and already outdated by the time another element acted on it.

By the later stages of the engagement, the force was spending most of its effort dealing with the consequences of the original mission rather than the original objective itself. The incident is useful because it shows how quickly one unexpected event can generate several additional problems, each of which competes for the same limited people and resources.

05Security Element Responding to a Compromised Diplomatic Facility

A small security element stationed at one facility received reports that a nearby diplomatic compound was under attack. The information coming from the compound was incomplete, friendly personnel were still believed to be inside, and the strength and organization of the attacking force were unclear.

The security element also had responsibility for its own location. Moving personnel toward the compromised compound would necessarily reduce the number of people available to protect the facility they were leaving.

That created a legitimate conflict between two responsibilities. Remaining in place preserved security at one location while conditions worsened at another. Moving toward the attack increased the chance of recovering personnel at the compromised compound but exposed the responding element and reduced security elsewhere.

When the security personnel eventually moved, they entered a situation that had already developed considerably. The compound had been penetrated, buildings were burning, friendly personnel were scattered, and armed individuals were present in the surrounding area without a clean or easily understood front line.

The responding group therefore had to build its understanding of the incident while already operating inside it. The immediate problem was no longer simply defending the compound. It had become a personnel recovery effort.

Once surviving personnel were located and moved, the mission changed again. The recovery became a withdrawal, and the withdrawal brought the same personnel back to another facility that now had to be defended.

The same small group moved through several different roles without a meaningful break between them. They began as a security element, became a response force, shifted into a recovery effort, withdrew under pressure, and then returned to a defensive role.

That progression is what makes the incident useful for discussion. The original mission description stopped being particularly relevant because the problem kept changing faster than personnel could be reorganized around it.

The people involved did not receive a fresh team for each new task. They carried fatigue, incomplete information, and the consequences of the previous phase directly into the next one.

06Investigative Convoy Ambush and Personnel Recovery

A small investigative team traveling with security personnel was attacked while moving through an urban corridor. The team was not primarily configured as an assault element, and the initial attack disrupted the convoy before everyone involved had a clear understanding of what was happening.

During the attack, one member of the group was taken. The remaining personnel were immediately dealing with two separate priorities: surviving the contact and preventing the captured member from being moved beyond their ability to recover him.

Those priorities pulled the group in different directions. Consolidating the surviving personnel would have provided better control and allowed more time to gather information, but delay also increased the likelihood that the captured member would be moved farther away.

The surviving group chose to continue after him. As they moved deeper into the urban environment, their control over the situation decreased. The opposing group had greater familiarity with the area, while the pursuing personnel were moving through terrain they did not fully understand.

The recovery effort eventually broke into several smaller confrontations. Different members of the team were handling different parts of the problem at the same time, and not everyone had a complete picture of what the others were doing.

That makes the incident useful because it presents a situation where neither obvious option is particularly good. Slowing down improves coordination but increases the chance of losing the captured person. Moving quickly preserves pressure but increases uncertainty and the likelihood that the responding group will become separated.

The value of the scenario is not in declaring one choice correct. It is in recognizing that some incidents present competing risks rather than a single safe option.

The loss of one person changed the priorities of the entire group and introduced a time constraint that shaped every decision afterward.

07Reconnaissance Element Attempting to Disengage in Mountainous Terrain

A four-person reconnaissance element operating in mountainous terrain was compromised and contacted by a substantially larger hostile force. Once that occurred, the original purpose of the mission became secondary to disengagement.

The terrain had initially helped conceal the team, but after contact it restricted their ability to leave. There were relatively few easy routes away, and movement required negotiating steep ground while attempting to remain together under pressure.

As the engagement continued, personnel were injured and communication became less reliable. The team's spacing changed, movement slowed, and the terrain itself caused additional physical injuries.

This produced a cycle that became increasingly difficult to break. Injuries reduced mobility, reduced mobility made it easier for the pursuing force to maintain pressure, and continued pressure created additional opportunities for more injuries.

The team was still capable of responding to immediate threats, but that did not necessarily solve the larger problem. Successful exchanges did not create a clear route out of the terrain or restore the mobility the group had lost.

Over time, the reconnaissance element became less like the four-person team that had begun the mission and more like a group of injured individuals attempting to remain together while moving away from a larger force.

The scenario is particularly useful for understanding the relationship between terrain and mission. The same ground that provided concealment during observation became restrictive once the team needed to withdraw quickly.

Terrain is not automatically an advantage simply because it offers cover or concealment. Its value depends on what the group needs to do at that particular moment.

08Small Police Assault Element Moving Through a Contested City

A small police assault element moved through a heavily damaged urban area where an organized hostile force controlled portions of the city. Threats could emerge from rooftops, damaged buildings, vehicles, roadblocks, explosive hazards, or individuals who were difficult to distinguish from the surrounding population.

Under those conditions, movement was not simply the space between engagements. Movement was part of the problem.

Vehicles provided speed and some protection, but they also tied the group to usable roads. Buildings could offer temporary security while simultaneously limiting visibility and exit options. Routes that appeared acceptable earlier could become unusable later as conditions changed.

The team repeatedly shifted between mounted and dismounted movement depending on what the environment allowed. Over time, casualties reduced the number of people available and vehicles were lost or abandoned.

What makes the incident useful is that the unit's capability did not disappear all at once. It eroded gradually.

When one person was lost, the team did not simply become smaller. Other people had to absorb that individual's responsibilities. Observation sectors changed, leadership responsibilities shifted, and tasks that had previously been divided among several people were now concentrated among fewer.

The same was true when a vehicle was lost. The effect was not limited to transportation. It changed how quickly the group could move, what equipment could be carried, and which routes remained practical.

By the later stages of the operation, the team was still attempting to perform much of the same work it had been doing at the beginning, but with substantially less margin for error.

That gradual redistribution of responsibilities is probably the most useful part of the scenario for a law-enforcement audience. In a small group, every person represents more than one set of hands. Each person also carries information, experience, equipment, and responsibilities that someone else may have to assume if that person is no longer available.

09Small Defensive Element Operating Inside a Penetrated Position

Personnel at an isolated outpost came under a coordinated attack from hostile forces occupying advantageous terrain around the position. During the engagement, portions of the perimeter were penetrated.

Once that occurred, the problem changed significantly. The defenders were no longer dealing only with an external threat. Friendly and hostile personnel were now operating inside the same general area.

The outpost stopped functioning as one continuous defensive position. Instead, it became a collection of smaller engagements involving isolated groups and individual fighting positions.

Some personnel were cut off from others. Some were trapped in areas they could not easily leave, while other members of the force attempted to move between positions without having a complete understanding of who controlled the ground between them.

Casualties and communication problems made that uncertainty worse. At that point, the formal perimeter mattered less than knowing where friendly personnel were located and whether those positions could still support each other.

The situation began to improve when surviving personnel started reconnecting those isolated locations. The larger conditions had not changed. The hostile force was still present, the terrain was still unfavorable, and the defenders had already suffered losses.

What changed was that small groups began restoring contact with one another and recovering usable portions of the position.

This is an important distinction because a perimeter can fail without every person inside it immediately becoming ineffective. Once the original boundary stops being meaningful, the problem may need to be understood in terms of people, occupied positions, and whether those positions can still communicate and support one another.

The incident is useful because it shows that the loss of organizational structure does not necessarily mean the complete loss of capability. In this case, capability was rebuilt locally before the larger position began functioning coherently again.

10Night Operation Inside a Walled Residential Compound

A specialized assault force entered a walled residential compound at night searching for a high-value individual whose exact location inside the property was unknown.

A significant problem developed during insertion when one aircraft became disabled. That immediately changed the extraction picture, but the ground element still had an unresolved objective inside the compound.

The property contained multiple structures, enclosed spaces, and occupants whose locations and intentions were not completely known. The team could not understand the entire environment at once. Information became available as personnel moved through the compound.

Each new area answered some questions while creating others. Personnel had to continue updating their understanding of where occupants were located, whether additional people remained elsewhere on the property, and whether the person they were looking for had already moved.

The disabled aircraft remained a serious issue throughout the operation, but it was not necessarily the most immediate issue at every moment. The ground force continued addressing the part of the situation directly in front of it while maintaining awareness that the aircraft and eventual withdrawal would still require attention.

That separation of problems is what makes the scenario useful. Several significant issues existed simultaneously, but they did not all require the same amount of attention at the same time.

The original plan changed almost immediately because of the aircraft problem and continued changing as new information became available inside the compound. The purpose of the operation, however, remained clear enough that personnel could continue working toward it despite those changes.

The incident provides a good example of the difference between preserving a plan and preserving an objective. The first became impossible fairly early. The second remained achievable.

11Two-Person Survival Movement After a Patrol Ambush

A patrol was ambushed in remote terrain and rapidly lost most of its personnel. Two individuals survived the immediate action, but one was seriously wounded and the other remained responsible for moving both of them away from the threat.

At that point, the patrol's original mission had effectively ended. The immediate objective became survival and movement.

The condition of the wounded person imposed a permanent limitation on everything that followed. The mobile survivor could have moved more quickly alone, but remaining with the casualty dramatically reduced the pace of movement.

That meant ordinary terrain became more difficult, distance became more significant, and periods of rest became unavoidable. Any new contact also carried greater consequences because there was almost no reserve capability left.

The most interesting part of the scenario is how the definition of success changes as the situation deteriorates.

At the beginning, success is tied to the original patrol mission. After the ambush, success becomes breaking contact. After the casualties, it becomes keeping two people alive and continuing to move.

Eventually, progress may be measured in relatively small distances rather than completion of any larger objective.

That reduction in scope is not necessarily indecision or defeatism. It can simply reflect an accurate understanding of what the remaining people are physically capable of accomplishing.

For law enforcement, that translates well beyond the military setting. Serious incidents sometimes force people to abandon the original objective and concentrate on preserving life, maintaining communication, or reaching a location where the situation can be reassessed.

The ability to recognize that the mission has changed can matter as much as the ability to continue pursuing the original one.

12Escort Element Withdrawing With Noncombatants

A small security element moving through hostile territory became responsible for a group of noncombatants while attempting to reach friendly control.

The presence of civilians changed the movement problem immediately. The security personnel could not move at whatever pace was most convenient for them. Their practical speed became the speed of the people they were responsible for protecting.

Route selection was affected in the same way. Terrain that presented little difficulty for trained personnel might be considerably harder for children, older adults, injured people, or civilians who were already exhausted.

As the possibility of contact increased, the security element had to divide its attention between the surrounding threat and the people it was trying to move.

Those responsibilities competed for personnel and attention. Someone helping civilians maintain movement was not giving the same level of attention to the surrounding environment. Someone focused entirely on security was less available to keep the protected group organized.

The security element could therefore perform well during an individual confrontation and still lose ground on the larger problem if the civilians stopped moving or became separated.

That is what makes the scenario different from a normal withdrawal. The security group's mobility was directly tied to people who did not possess the same training, equipment, physical ability, or understanding of the situation.

For a law-enforcement audience, the parallel is straightforward. Evacuations and protective movements rarely involve only trained personnel. Officers may be responsible for children, injured civilians, elderly people, intoxicated individuals, or people who simply do not understand what is happening.

The protected group is not separate from the tactical problem. Its condition and behavior help define what options are realistically available.

13Reconnaissance Element Pinned Inside a Civilian Structure

A small reconnaissance element occupied a civilian residence in order to observe activity in the surrounding urban area. Initially, the structure served that purpose well by providing concealment, elevation, and separation from activity outside.

Once the team's presence was discovered, the purpose of the building changed. The same location that had been useful for observation now had to be defended.

After personnel were wounded, it changed again. The structure became a place where casualties had to be treated while the remaining members of the team continued monitoring the surrounding threat and coordinating outside assistance.

That progression is what makes the incident useful. Nothing about the building itself changed, but the team's requirements changed dramatically.

As casualties accumulated, personnel who had previously been available for security tasks were redirected toward medical care, communication, and casualty movement. The number of people physically inside the structure may not have changed much, but the number available to watch approaches and respond to threats had been reduced.

Eventually, remaining in the building stopped offering the same advantages it had provided earlier. A position selected for observation had limited options for moving casualties and became increasingly restrictive once the team needed to leave.

The broader point is that a location should not be considered good or bad in the abstract. Its value depends on what personnel need from it at a particular moment.

A building that is excellent for observation may be poor for evacuation. A location that offers concealment may have limited exits. A position that works well while nobody knows you are there may become far less useful after compromise.

For law enforcement, that is a familiar issue. Houses, apartments, businesses, vehicles, stairwells, and parking structures can all change from useful locations into restrictive ones as the purpose of the response changes.

14Patrol Immobilized by Explosive Hazards During Casualty Recovery

A small patrol moving through open terrain encountered an explosive device that immediately caused a serious casualty. Personnel naturally attempted to reach the injured individual, but the casualty was located inside an area where additional explosive hazards were believed to be present.

That created a direct conflict between medical urgency and movement safety.

The need to reach the casualty encouraged personnel to move quickly. The possibility of additional devices meant that careless movement could create more casualties.

As more people entered the area attempting to assist, additional explosive hazards were encountered. What began as one injured person gradually developed into a larger casualty problem.

Unlike many of the other incidents in this collection, there was no visible opponent whose actions could be observed. The threat was embedded in the environment.

That removed many of the responses people normally associate with an armed confrontation. Speed was not automatically helpful. Adding more personnel was not automatically helpful. Aggressive action could make the problem worse rather than better.

Previously traveled ground became valuable because it represented information. Unknown ground represented uncertainty.

Every additional casualty also changed the requirements of the recovery effort. Personnel who entered intending to help someone else could themselves become people who needed assistance.

The scenario is useful because it demonstrates how strong emotional pressure can conflict with the safest available course of action. Seeing an injured person naturally creates an immediate desire to reach that person.

In some environments, however, moving without enough information can increase the number of people who need rescue.

For law enforcement, the specific hazard may be different, but the underlying issue is familiar. Officers sometimes encounter scenes where the first visible casualty is not the entire problem. The conditions that injured that person may still be present, and the desire to immediately help has to be balanced against the possibility of creating additional victims.

15Armed Criminal Group Contacted During an Urban Escape

A heavily armed criminal group attempting to leave an urban area became trapped in traffic while a pursuing law-enforcement element closed on its position.

The offenders recognized that a confrontation was developing before most of the surrounding civilian population did, but the physical environment limited what they could do with that information.

Traffic occupied the roadway and civilian vehicles left little room for movement. The offenders could not simply accelerate away, while pursuing officers also had to operate around uninvolved people and vehicles.

Once the confrontation began, the offenders' objective remained escape. Staying in place became increasingly disadvantageous because every additional delay created more opportunity for law-enforcement resources to arrive.

When vehicle movement became impractical, the remaining offenders transitioned to movement on foot.

That solved the immediate problem of being trapped inside the vehicle, but it removed something else the vehicle had been providing: cohesion.

While everyone remained in the same vehicle, the group had a common location and generally shared the same understanding of what was happening. Once they left it, individuals began moving through the environment at different speeds and with different information.

Casualties made that separation worse. The group became progressively smaller and less coordinated until the withdrawal no longer functioned as one organized effort.

The useful distinction is between mobility and firepower. The offenders retained significant ability to engage pursuing officers, but that did not mean they had somewhere useful to go.

Being able to create temporary space is not the same as recovering a viable route out of the area.

For law enforcement, that distinction matters when looking at armed offenders who are attempting to flee rather than occupy ground. Their behavior may be driven less by an effort to defeat responding officers than by an effort to recover enough mobility to continue escaping.

Understanding that objective can help make sense of movement that might otherwise appear inconsistent or disorganized.

Closing Observation

When the Original Plan Stops Matching the Incident

Across all of these scenarios, the common issue is not a particular weapon, tactic, or type of unit. It is the way small groups respond when the assumptions behind the original plan begin to fail.

A group may start with a clear objective, a known number of personnel, working vehicles, established communications, and an expected route. Then an aircraft goes down, a vehicle becomes trapped, a member is captured, a position is penetrated, a casualty cannot move, or a route becomes unusable.

The important part is that these problems rarely remain isolated.

A casualty reduces manpower, but it may also require medical attention, transportation, additional security, and somebody else to assume the injured person's responsibilities. A disabled vehicle changes mobility, but it may also change available equipment, communication, and the ability to move casualties. A lost route affects movement, but it can also separate personnel and disrupt the information available to different parts of the group.

This matters even more in small teams because each person represents a relatively large portion of the group's total capability. Losing one person from a four-person group is not simply a twenty-five percent reduction in headcount. The remaining three people also have to absorb whatever that fourth person had been responsible for.

The same principle applies to law enforcement incidents. An officer who moves to provide medical care is still present, but for that period he may no longer be available for another task. An officer assigned to a prisoner, witness, evacuation, perimeter point, or casualty has effectively changed roles, and the rest of the group has to adjust around that.

That is why these scenarios are useful to discuss without reducing them to whether somebody made the "right" or "wrong" move.

The more useful questions are usually what information was available at the time, what the group believed the problem was, what changed after the decision was made, and whether everyone recognized that change at roughly the same time.

In many of these incidents, the people involved continue trying to execute a plan that made sense only a few minutes earlier. The groups that remain functional are generally the ones that recognize when the problem has changed and begin reorganizing around the situation they actually have rather than the one they expected.

That is probably the common thread worth carrying through the entire collection. The shooting may be the most visible part of an incident, but the harder problem is often maintaining a shared understanding of what is happening while the situation, the available personnel, and the objective are all changing at the same time.

Discussion material, not doctrine. Incidents are described in general terms for small-group decision-making study.
