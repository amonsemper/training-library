Analogue Comms

Keeping local, regional, and long-distance communication going when the internet, the cell network, or the power grid is down: radio simplex, field telephones and wired Morse, HF radio, receive-only news and weather, independent power, and the procedures that make it all work.

This is a reference, not legal advice. Radio transmission in the U.S. is licensed and regulated by the FCC: check which service each radio is authorized for, get the license your service requires, and verify the current rules before you transmit. Receiving is generally unrestricted; transmitting is not.

01Design principles

The goal is communication that keeps working when the infrastructure doesn't: no internet, no cell towers, no grid power. Every choice below follows from a few rules.

Direct links first

Prefer links that depend on nothing in between: radio in simplex, one set talking straight to another, and field wire you installed yourself.

Own your power

Give each essential station its own battery and a way to recharge it that doesn't need the grid.

Two ways to every contact

Keep at least two different ways to reach each important contact, where practical, so one failure doesn't cut them off.

Paper, not screens

Keep printed channel plans, operating instructions, contact schedules, maps, and logs where every operator can reach them.

Test in your terrain

Manufacturer range claims are not planning figures. Find out what actually works by testing with your own equipment, on your own ground.

Stay legal

Follow the FCC licensing and equipment-certification rules for every radio service you transmit on.

03Handheld radios in simplex

Handheld radios such as Baofengs work without internet, cell towers, GPS, or grid power. In simplex, one radio transmits straight to another on the same frequency. All they need is charged batteries, matching frequencies and settings, and a usable radio path between them. A repeater extends range, but it is optional, and it is one more thing that can fail.

Range planning

EnvironmentIllustrative handheld-to-handheld range
Dense woods, uneven terrainAbout 0.5 to 2 miles
Rolling rural terrainAbout 1 to 5 miles
Open groundAbout 3 to 8 miles
Favorable elevated-to-elevated path10 to 20+ miles possible

These are rough examples, not guaranteed performance. Real range depends heavily on line of sight, hills, vegetation, antenna height, interference, and how the radio is set up. Survey the area you need to cover with the real equipment.

Handheld checklist

Know your radios

Write down the exact model of every unit and check which radio service each one is authorized to transmit on.

Program for simplex

Program compatible, authorized simplex channels and don't build the plan around repeaters. Label every radio and keep a printed channel plan that matches it.

Walk the coverage

Test from the house, the workshop, the property boundaries, the roads, and the low spots. The low spots are where it fails.

Antenna before power

A properly installed, elevated base antenna usually buys more range than a higher-power handheld. Fix the antenna first.

Batteries and charging

Keep spare batteries and a 12 V or solar-backed way to charge them.

Assume you're heard

Use plain-language procedures and assume anyone with a receiver can hear your traffic. Radio is not private.

04Licensing

Common UV-5R-type radios are usually amateur-service transceivers. They are not automatically authorized to transmit on GMRS, FRS, or MURS, even though they can tune those frequencies.

05Wired communications

A wire you lay yourself can't be jammed, doesn't depend on propagation, and doesn't need a phone company.

Field telephones

Military-style field telephones such as the TA-312/PT set up a point-to-point voice circuit over two-conductor field wire, with no phone company or internet service involved. Turning the crank rings the other station; internal batteries typically power the speech circuit.

Wired telegraph

A wired Morse circuit needs a key, a battery, the wire and its return circuit, and a sounder or buzzer at the far end. A Morse key by itself can't reach a distant station: it's only the switch.

06HF and Morse

An HF transceiver with a suitable antenna can reach regional or long-distance contacts by SSB voice or CW Morse, well past anything a handheld can do. Range and reliability change with frequency, time of day, season, and ionospheric conditions, so a contact that works at night may not work at noon.

NVIS (near-vertical incidence skywave) techniques send the signal almost straight up so it comes down over a wide area around you, which can fill the gap between handheld range and long-distance skip for some regional contacts.

A General-class amateur license is the practical target for broad U.S. HF voice privileges; check the band-specific rules. CW Morse gets through on weaker signals and simpler equipment than voice, which is why it's worth learning.

07Receive-only information

Keep a standalone AM/FM/shortwave receiver and a NOAA weather-alert radio. A scanner can add local situational awareness where those transmissions continue. None of these need the internet to receive, but all of them depend on the stations they listen to staying on the air.

08Power and physical resilience

One standard

Standardize fixed stations on fused 12 V DC where practical, so radios, chargers, and lights share one power system.

Battery and solar

Use a suitably rated LiFePO4 battery with a compatible charger and a solar charge controller.

Handheld spares

Keep independent spare handheld batteries and the charging cables to go with them.

Antennas and lightning

Install outdoor antennas with proper grounding, lightning protection, and safe cable routing.

Protected documents

Store printed documentation and essential spares somewhere protected that you can still get to.

09Operating procedures

Equipment matters, but prearranged contacts, procedures, and regular tests are what make a working network.

Agree in advance

Set primary and backup channels, scheduled check-in times, contact priorities, and simple message formats before you need them.

Log it

Keep written logs of contacts, times, and what was passed.

Train everyone

Every intended operator should be able to power up, find the right channel, and pass a message without help.

Test it dark

Run a monthly test with phones and internet deliberately unavailable, and test on battery power only from time to time.

10Readiness checklist

Work down the list in order: the first steps make what you already own reliable before you buy anything new.

#Step
1Inventory your handheld models, batteries, chargers, and antennas.
2Verify which service each radio is authorized for, and get operator licenses.
3Set up and test a local simplex radio network.
4Improve base-station antenna placement and weather protection.
5Build a two-station wired field-telephone circuit where it's useful.
6Add independent AM/FM/shortwave and NOAA weather reception.
7Set up fused battery and solar-backed power.
8Add CB if nearby vehicle or community contacts justify it.
9Work toward an amateur HF license, an HF station, and Morse proficiency.
10Prepare passive signaling and printed message procedures.
11Run a full off-grid communications exercise and write down the coverage gaps.

Priority order

First

Make the handheld radios you already have reliable in simplex: batteries, matched programming, and an elevated base antenna.

Second

Set up a fixed wired link and receive-only news and weather.

Third

Add HF radio and CW for longer-range contacts.
