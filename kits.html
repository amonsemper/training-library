Kits and Go-Bags

What to pack and where to keep it: a go-bag for leaving in a hurry, a home kit for riding it out, a vehicle kit, first-aid kits sized from pocket to household, and a schedule that keeps them all from quietly expiring.

A checklist is a starting point, not a finished kit. Pack for your own household, climate, medical needs, and plans, and practice with what you carry. A kit you can't lift or don't know how to use won't help.

01Principles

Layers, not one bag

Everyday carry on you, a go-bag by the door, a vehicle kit, and a home kit. Each one covers a different situation, and together they overlap.

Weight is the limit

Pack the go-bag, then walk a mile with it. As a guide, keep it under about 20% of your body weight. Water is the heaviest item and the most important.

Skills over stuff

Every item should be something you've used. A tourniquet you've never applied, a filter you've never run, or a radio you've never programmed is dead weight.

One per person

Each adult carries their own essentials. Kids can carry a small pack with water, a snack, a jacket, a whistle, and a card with contact details.

03Home kit (two weeks)

For staying put through an outage, storm, or quarantine. It lives in one closet or bin so everyone knows where it is.

Water and food

Two weeks of each (see Water and Food Storage), manual can opener, paper plates and utensils.

Light and power

LED lanterns and flashlights with spare batteries, a power station or generator plan (see Off-Grid Configurations), and a battery or crank radio.

Heat and shelter

Sleeping bags or extra blankets, plastic sheeting and duct tape to close off rooms or broken windows, and an indoor-safe heat plan.

Sanitation

Buckets, heavy trash bags, cat litter, bleach, soap, and hand sanitizer.

Safety

Fire extinguisher, smoke and battery-powered CO alarms, a wrench for the gas valve (only if you smell gas; let the utility turn it back on), and work gloves.

Medical

The household first-aid kit, a supply of prescriptions, and anything powered (CPAP, oxygen) on a backup power plan.

04Vehicle kit

Getting moving again

Jump pack or cables, tire inflator and plug kit, tow strap, a small shovel, traction mats or kitty litter in winter.

Staying put safely

Water, snacks, blanket or sleeping bag, warm clothes, flashlight, first-aid kit, phone charger, reflective triangles or flares.

Getting home on foot

Comfortable shoes, a small pack, water and a filter, a paper map, a light, and a rain layer: for when the car stays behind.

Keep the tank half full

Fuel stations need power to pump. Don't let the tank drop below half before a storm or in winter.

05First-aid kits

Two different kits do two different jobs: one stops bleeding that kills in minutes, the other handles everything else. See Field Medical for how to use them.

06Documents and money

Copies of what matters

IDs, insurance policies, deeds or lease, medical and prescription lists, vaccination records, and pet records: paper copies in a waterproof bag plus an encrypted USB drive.

Contacts on paper

A printed list of family phone numbers, an out-of-area contact, and your meeting places. Phones die. See the printable family plan card.

Cash

Small bills. Card readers and ATMs need power and a network.

Spare keys

House and vehicle keys in the go-bag.

07Keeping kits current

Check every kit twice a year. An easy reminder: when the clocks change.

Rotate

Water, food, batteries, and medications by their dates. Use the old ones at home.

Swap for the season

Winter layers and hand warmers in for the cold months; extra water and sun protection in for summer.

Re-fit the people

Kids outgrow clothes and shoes, prescriptions change, and new family members or pets need their own items.

Test the gear

Charge power banks and radios, run the filter, check the headlamp, and make sure the stove still lights.
