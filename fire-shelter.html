Fire and Shelter

Staying warm and dry when you can't rely on a heated house: the order of survival priorities, picking and building a shelter, starting and keeping a fire, putting it out properly, and dealing with cold and wet.

This is a reference, not wilderness training. Check fire restrictions and burn bans before lighting anything outdoors; a campfire that escapes can kill people and destroy homes. Never burn anything indoors except in a fireplace or stove built for it, and keep a CO alarm in any space you heat.

01Priorities

A common rule of thumb, the rule of threes, puts the order in perspective: in harsh conditions people can last roughly three hours without shelter, three days without water, and three weeks without food. The numbers vary widely, but the order is the point: in cold, wind, or rain, shelter comes before everything except stopping serious bleeding.

Get out of the weather

Wind and wet pull heat from the body far faster than still, dry cold.

Get off the ground

Cold ground draws heat out by contact. Insulation underneath matters more than a roof overhead.

Then fire

Fire warms, dries clothes, boils water, signals, and lifts morale. Build the shelter first, while you still have daylight and energy.

Stay found

If you're lost, staying put near your vehicle or a clearing usually beats wandering. Tell someone your plan before you go out.

02Picking a site

Avoid low ground

Valley floors and hollows collect cold air at night and flood in rain. A spot a little up a slope is often several degrees warmer.

Look up

Dead branches and leaning dead trees ("widowmakers") fall without warning, especially in wind or snow.

Avoid dry creek beds and washes

Flash floods arrive from rain miles away.

Use the land

A rock face, fallen log, or dense evergreens block wind. Face the opening away from the wind.

ShelterNeedsGood for
Tarp A-frameTarp, ridgeline cord between two trees, stakesRain from both sides; the most versatile setup
Tarp lean-toTarp, ridgeline, stakesWind from one direction, with a fire in front to reflect heat in
Emergency bivyA reflective bivy sackLight and fast; keeps a sleeping person dry and warmer. Expect condensation.
Debris hutA ridge pole, sticks, and a thick pile of leaves or pine needlesNo gear at all. Leaves at least an arm's length deep on top and a thick bed underneath.
VehicleThe car you're inOut of wind and wet. Crack a window, keep the exhaust clear of snow, and run the engine only in short spells to avoid carbon monoxide.

Small beats big: a shelter just big enough for you is far easier to keep warm.

04Building a fire

Fire needs heat, fuel, and oxygen. Most failures come from too-big fuel too early, damp fuel, or smothering a young flame.

StageSizeExamples
TinderFluffy, catches a sparkCotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint, birch bark, fatwood shavings, dry grass, char cloth
KindlingPencil lead to pencil thickDead twigs snapped from standing trees (they're drier than ground wood), split sticks
FuelThumb to wrist thick, then largerDead, dry wood that snaps cleanly; add gradually

Gather it all first

Collect twice as much of each size as you think you need before you strike anything.

Teepee or log cabin

A teepee of kindling over tinder catches fast; a log-cabin stack burns longer and makes good coals for cooking.

Wet weather

Split wood to reach the dry core, shave feather sticks, and build on a platform of sticks so the fire isn't sitting on wet ground.

The fire site

Use an existing fire ring where there is one. Otherwise clear a 10-foot circle down to bare soil, away from overhanging branches, tents, and dry grass.

05Ignition

Lighter

The easiest and most reliable. Carry two. Warm a butane lighter in a pocket in the cold; cold butane won't light well.

Ferro rod

Works wet and in the cold, thousands of strikes. Pull the rod back against a fixed striker so you don't knock your tinder apart.

Storm-proof matches

Burn in wind and even after being dunked. Keep them in a waterproof case with the striker.

Practice

Light a fire with each method in the backyard, in rain, with cold hands, before you need to.

06Putting it out

The Forest Service method: drown, stir, feel. Pour water over every ember, stir the ashes, pour again, and feel with the back of your hand. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave. Never bury a fire with dirt alone: it can smolder for days and flare up.

07Cold and wet

Dress in layers

A base layer that wicks sweat (wool or synthetic), an insulating layer (fleece or down), and a wind and rain shell. Shed layers before you sweat; sweat-soaked clothes chill you later.

Cotton kills

Wet cotton loses almost all its insulation and dries slowly. Avoid it in cold or wet conditions.

Hypothermia signs

Shivering, fumbling hands, slurred speech, confusion, and drowsiness. Get the person dry, insulated from the ground, out of wind, and give warm sweet drinks if they're alert. See Field Medical.

Keep extremities warm

Hat, gloves, and dry socks. Change into dry socks before sleeping.

08Shelter at home

In a winter outage, treat the house like a big shelter.

Pick one room

A small interior room with few windows, ideally on the sunny side. Close the doors to the rest of the house.

Seal it

Blankets or plastic over windows, towels along door gaps. A tent set up inside that room holds body heat for sleeping.

Safe heat only

A wood stove, a heater rated for indoor use, or the low-power options on the Off-Grid page. Never a grill, camp stove, or gas oven.

Protect the pipes

If the heat will be off for days in freezing weather, shut off the water main and drain the pipes to prevent burst lines.
