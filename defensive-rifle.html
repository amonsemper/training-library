Defensive Rifle Shooting

Using a carbine to protect life: legal limits, setup, ready positions, mechanical offset at close range, shooting from cover, moving off the line of attack, malfunctions, transitions, low light, and home-defense considerations.

Built on TC 3-22.9, common defensive carbine curricula, and general U.S. self-defense law. This is training information, not legal advice. Laws differ by state; learn yours from a qualified attorney or instructor. Figures assume a 5.56 mm carbine with a 50/200-yard zero and 2.6-inch sight height.

01Legal and ethical limits

Most U.S. jurisdictions allow deadly force only when you reasonably believe it's necessary to prevent imminent death or serious bodily harm to yourself or another innocent person. A rifle doesn't change that standard. See the Defensive Handgun page for the full framework.

Ability

The attacker has the means to kill or cause serious injury.

Opportunity

They're in a position to use it now.

Jeopardy

Their actions show intent to use it. A reasonable person would believe the threat is real.

A rifle round can travel through several walls and well beyond your property. You're responsible for every round, including misses and pass-throughs.

02Mindset and awareness

Awareness first

The best defensive outcome is one where you never need the rifle: you noticed early, avoided, left, locked the door, or called for help. The rifle is for when those options are gone.

Decide in advance

Know your home's layout, where family members sleep, your safe room, and the lines of fire that would cross a family member's room. Decide those things now, not at 3 a.m.

05Mechanical offset

The bore sits below the optic. Up close, the bullet hasn't risen to the line of sight yet, so it hits below where the dot is. At room distances this is about 2–2½ inches. On a large target it doesn't matter; on a small, precise shot it can mean a miss.

For a precise close shot, hold the dot about 2 inches high. Practice it on small targets at 3–7 yards until it's automatic.

06Shooting from cover

Cover stops rounds; concealment only hides you. Using cover well means staying back from it, so you see around the edge with as little of your body exposed as possible, and so your muzzle doesn't poke past the edge where it can be seen or grabbed.

Exposure at a corner

Rules

  • Stand back from cover, 3–6 feet when space allows.
  • Work the edge like slicing the pie, in small steps.
  • Shoot around the side, not over the top, when possible; less head exposure.
  • Switch shoulders to match the side of cover if you've trained it. It reduces exposure on the support-side corner.
  • Remember mechanical offset when shooting just over or past a low edge: the dot can be clear while the bore is still behind the cover.

What counts as cover

  • Usually cover: engine blocks, wheel hubs, thick concrete, brick and block walls (for a few hits), earth berms.
  • Concealment only: car doors and body panels, interior drywall walls, wooden fences, hedges, furniture.

07Movement and assessment

A stationary person is the easiest target there is. When a threat appears, moving off the line of attack, ideally toward cover, forces the attacker to react to you. After the threat stops, assess: is it actually over, is anyone else involved, is anyone hurt?

Off the X, to cover, then assess
  • Move laterally or diagonally away, not straight backward. Most people can't move backward as fast as an attacker moves forward.
  • If you shoot, shoot until the threat stops, then stop. Each round must be justified.
  • Come off the sights to see the whole threat: hands, weapon, movement.
  • Scan left, right, and behind. Break tunnel vision by moving your head, not just your eyes.

Stuck caseBolt locked closed on an empty caseOut of the fight; needs a cleaning rod. Transition.
Clear the rifle or transition?

If you carry a pistol and the threat is close, transitioning is usually faster than clearing anything beyond a tap-rack. At longer range or behind solid cover, clearing the rifle may be better since the pistol is harder to hit with at distance.

09Reloads

Emergency reload

  1. Bolt locked back, empty magazine.
  2. Press the magazine release as the support hand gets a fresh magazine.
  3. Insert, tug to confirm it's seated.
  4. Hit the bolt release or slingshot the charging handle.
  5. Back on target; reassess.

Tactical reload

During a lull, from behind cover: take a fresh magazine in the support hand, remove the partial, insert the fresh one, stow the partial. Only do this when you have the time; a gun with rounds in it is better than a gun in the middle of a reload.

10Low light

  • Identify before you shoot. Low light is where misidentification happens. The light's main job is to see what's in someone's hands.
  • Momentary use. Light on to search or identify, off, then move. Constant-on tells everyone where you are.
  • Use the edge of the beam (the spill) to search without pointing the muzzle at what you're looking at.
  • Know your switch by feel. Practice activation from each ready position.

11Home defense

Plan

  • Default plan: gather family to a safe room, call 911, hold a defensible position covering the entry. Don't search the house unless someone needs you.
  • Know where every family member's room is relative to your likely lines of fire.
  • Store the rifle so you can get to it quickly and unauthorized people (including children) can't. A quick-access safe or rack with a lock.

Over-penetration

Typical interior walls are two layers of drywall and offer almost no resistance. Testing by independent groups commonly shows lightweight 5.56 mm bullets fragmenting in wallboard and passing through fewer walls than many 9 mm or buckshot loads, but they still pass through several. No common defensive round is wall-safe. Plan lines of fire, not cartridges.

Short barrels and hearing

Indoors, a 16-inch or shorter carbine is loud enough to cause permanent hearing damage in one shot. A suppressor, where legal, or electronic hearing protection kept with the rifle reduces it. Plan for disorientation if neither is available.

13After an incident

  1. Stay behind cover and keep watching until you're sure it's over.
  2. Call 911: your location, that you were attacked, that someone is hurt and needs medical help, your description, and that you're armed.
  3. Put the rifle down or sling it before police arrive. Have empty hands visible. Follow every command.
  4. Give officers the basic facts: you were attacked, point out evidence and witnesses. Then say you'll cooperate fully after speaking with your attorney.
  5. Get medical attention for yourself. Adrenaline hides injuries.
