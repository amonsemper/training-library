Recon & CQB Primer

Reconnaissance, urban terrain analysis, building recon, movement between buildings, entry, room-to-room clearing, and vehicle route assessment, planning, avoidance, and engagements for a four-person team. Each drill has an animated sand table you can play, pause, slow down, and scrub.

Drawn from U.S. Army doctrine (FM/ATTP 3-06.11, ATP 3-21.8, ATP 3-20.98). Distances are approximate and given in feet. Rehearse with qualified instructors, progress from walk-throughs to dry runs before anything live, and follow your range or facility's safety rules.

Muzzle discipline: no crossing fields of fire

No member's sector or muzzle ever covers a teammate. Sectors point outward and away from each other. When a teammate moves into your sector, your muzzle comes off them until they're clear. 01Recon fundamentals

Recon answers the leader's questions before the team commits. You go to see the ground, the structures, and the people, and you report what you find quickly and accurately. Recon elements avoid a decisive fight. Once they're pinned down, they stop collecting.

Seven fundamentals

  1. Ensure continuous reconnaissance before, during, and after the operation
  2. Don't keep recon assets in reserve; idle recon gathers nothing
  3. Orient on the recon objective: the terrain, the building, or the threat you were sent for
  4. Report rapidly and accurately; old information loses value fast
  5. Retain freedom of maneuver; always have a way out
  6. Gain and maintain contact once found, by eyes or sensors
  7. Develop the situation rapidly so the leader can decide

SLLS at every halt

  • Stop in cover, weapons oriented outward
  • Look near to far, scanning in overlapping strips about 150 feet deep
  • Listen for engines, voices, doors, dogs, generators
  • Smell for cooking, fuel, smoke, tobacco, fresh paint

Hold 1–5 minutes so your senses adjust and the area settles after your arrival. Longer at night.

Send what you saw, not what you think it means. If you add an assessment, label it as one ("assess they are preparing to leave"). Report negative information too. "No movement on sides 2 and 3 for 40 minutes" is useful.

02Planning the recon

A recon without clear questions comes back with a lot of observations and few answers. Start from what the leader needs to know, then plan routes, observation points, and timings to answer it.

Information requirements

Write the specific questions the recon must answer. Each one should be answerable by observation.

  • Which entry point has the shortest covered approach?
  • How many people are in the building, and on which floors?
  • Do adjacent buildings overlook the approach?
  • Which way do the exterior doors swing?

METT-TC check

  • Mission: what the recon is for and when the answers are due
  • Enemy or threat: what's known, what's likely
  • Terrain and weather: routes, cover, light, noise
  • Troops: who goes, who provides security
  • Time: hard time to be back or to report
  • Civilian considerations: who else is in the area

Leader's recon and the contingency plan

When the leader leaves the team to look at the objective, they issue a five-point contingency plan before departing. The acronym is GOTWA.

ElementExample
GWhere the leader is going"Moving to the hedge line at the NW corner of the objective."
OOthers going along"Taking #3 for security."
TTime the leader will be gone"Back in 30 minutes."
WWhat to do if the leader doesn't return"After 45 minutes, move to the alternate rally point and report."
AActions on contact, for both elements"If we're hit, we break contact east. If you're hit, move to the alternate rally point."

Actions at the objective: the fan method

The team halts at an objective rally point (ORP) out of sight and sound of the objective. A security element holds the ORP while a recon pair moves out on a series of routes, like the ribs of a fan, to observation points (OPs) that see different faces of the objective. They return to the ORP between legs to report and update the plan.

Fan method area recon

Fan

One pair, several legs from the ORP. Slow, but security stays concentrated. Good when the team is small.

Converging routes

Separate pairs move on different routes and link up at a point past the objective. Faster, but control and fratricide risk go up.

Box

Pairs move around the objective's perimeter and link up at a far point. Covers the whole outer edge. Needs more people.

Observation posts and hide sites

  • Pick a spot that sees the objective but isn't the obvious place to look from. Avoid isolated landmarks, rooftops with nothing else on them, and the only window that faces the target.
  • In buildings, observe from back in the room, away from the window, so your silhouette and any light stay inside. A gap in a curtain or a broken pane is better than an open window.
  • Two people minimum: one observes, one records and provides security. Rotate every 20–30 minutes to keep attention sharp.
  • Keep a running log with times, then build the range card and building sketch from it.

03Urban terrain

Cities are three-dimensional. Threats and observation can come from below the street, at street level, from inside upper floors, and from rooftops. Plan sectors for every level, not just the one you're standing on.

04Building recon

Observe the target building from stand-off before anyone approaches it. Build a sketch from at least two angles, ideally all four sides. What you note here drives the entry point, the route in, and the clearing order.

Structure

  • Construction type (see section 03)
  • Number of floors, basement windows, roof access, fire escapes
  • Door swing direction, hinge side, locks, bars, screen doors
  • Likely floor plan from window spacing, chimneys, plumbing vents, meters
  • Where stairs are likely, from stairwell windows offset between floors

Surroundings

  • Covered and concealed approaches; dead space you can't see into
  • Windows and rooftops in adjacent buildings that overlook your route
  • Danger areas: streets, alleys, parking lots, open yards
  • Fences, gates, dogs, motion lights, cameras
  • Pattern of life: who comes and goes, when lights change, vehicles present

Choosing the entry point

  • Top down when possible. Entering at the roof or upper floor and clearing down puts gravity and stairs on your side. Threats pushed down tend to leave the building, where an outer cordon can deal with them.
  • Avoid the obvious door when another option exists. Front doors are the opening most likely to be watched or blocked.
  • Shortest covered approach wins at ground level, with an overwatch position that can see the entry point and the windows above it.
  • Have an alternate. If the primary is locked, blocked, or compromised, the team moves to the alternate on a known signal without stopping in the open to discuss it.

05Building to building

Open ground between buildings is where a team is most exposed. Split into two buddy pairs: one pair covers while the other moves. Every bound ends in cover with a new overwatch set before the other pair leaves. Plan the routes so the moving pair never passes through the covering pair's sectors.

Bounding overwatch across a danger area

Moving along buildings

  • Stay 12–18 inches off the wall. Rounds that strike a wall tend to travel along it.
  • Use the shadow side of the street. Avoid silhouetting against lit walls.
  • Keep a low profile at corners and openings; let the lead set the pace.
  • Assign one person high (windows, roofs) and one low (basements, vehicles).

Danger areas

  • Linear: streets, alleys, rail lines. Cross at the narrowest point, ideally not at an intersection.
  • Open areas: lots, parks, yards. Bound across, or go around if time allows.
  • Intersections: exposed from four directions. Avoid if possible.
  • Check the far side visually before anyone crosses; cross fast, then set security on the far side.

Corners

Round a corner the same way you slice a doorway (section 07): back off the corner, then step out in small increments so you see a thin wedge at a time. When two people take a corner together, one can work low (kneeling) and one high (standing) so both can see without one masking the other. Agree on who goes low before you get there.

06Entry and the stack

The team lines up against the wall beside the entry, on the same side, in the order they'll go in. #1 owns the door. The doorway itself is the fatal funnel: anyone inside watching that opening has it covered, so nobody stops in it, stands in front of it, or waits in line inside it.

Red wedges are what the two corners inside can see through the doorway. Where they overlap is the fatal funnel. The timer counts how long #1 spends in it.

Center-fed door

Door near the middle of the wall. The room opens to both sides on entry, so #1 and #2 split left and right.

Corner-fed door

Door within a few feet of a corner. One side is a short wall and a hard corner; the other is the full length of the room. #1 takes the long side, #2 the corner.

Doors

  • Hinge side and swing decide where you stack. Stack on the knob side when you can, so the door opens away from the stack and doesn't block #1.
  • Inward-opening doors are pushed open and followed through. Outward-opening doors need someone to pull and hold them, which slows the entry and leaves that person in a bad spot.
  • Try the handle quietly before anything else. An unlocked door is the fastest and quietest option.
  • Locked doors call for a breach. Breaching methods are grouped as mechanical (tools, rams), ballistic, thermal, and explosive; the last three are specialist tasks that need specific training and authorization.

Entry techniques

TechniqueWhenHow it differs
DynamicHigh threat, speed and surprise available, little need to discriminateTeam flows in without pausing at the door. Each member takes a point of domination. Relies on speed, surprise, and violence of action.
Deliberate / limited penetrationThreat likely, but noncombatants may be present or the room is unknownClear as much as possible from outside by slicing the pie, then enter only to clear what can't be seen. Slower, more control.
Stealth / methodicalSurprise is still intact and worth keepingQuiet, slow movement with no talking. Clear by observation, move only when necessary. Speed comes only after compromise.

Signals in the stack. Keep them silent and physical: a squeeze on the shoulder or thigh passed forward means "ready." #1 moves when #2 signals ready, or #1 can initiate. Agree on the system before the first rehearsal and use it every time.

07Slicing the pie

When a single operator or a deliberate team needs to see into a room before entering, they clear it from outside in thin slices. Stand back from the opening and step laterally in a curve. The door frame limits what you see, and it also limits who can see you.

Slicing a doorway from the hall
  • Distance from the opening gives you time and narrows your exposure. Stay 6–10 feet off it when the space allows.
  • Eyes, muzzle, and feet move together. Small lateral steps, not leaning.
  • Corners nearest the door wall show up last, if at all. Those are the spots you can't clear from outside, and the reason a team still enters.
  • If you see a threat, you've also been exposed to it. Decide in advance whether you engage, step back, or call it.

08Two-person entry

A pair can clear a small room when the rest of the team is holding security elsewhere, or when that's all there is. The pattern is the same as the first half of a four-person entry: #1 goes one way, #2 goes the other, and both work along the walls to points of domination.

Two-person entry, center-fed

Strengths

  • Fast, simple, leaves two people for security
  • Good for small rooms, closets, bathrooms

Limits

  • No one follows to cover the center of a large room
  • A casualty leaves one person alone in the room
  • Rooms over about 15 by 15 feet favor a four-person entry

09Four-person entry

A dynamic entry relies on speed, surprise, and controlled violence of action. Each member flows through the door and takes the direction opposite the person ahead of them, moving along the wall to a point of domination. Sectors overlap and nobody crosses in front of a teammate's muzzle.

PositionMovesImmediate threat areaEnds at
#1Through the door, direction of least resistance or as rehearsedThe corner on their side of the entry wallDeep along the wall, about 3 feet short of the far corner
#2Opposite of #1The near corner on their sideAlong the wall on the opposite side from #1
#3Opposite of #2 (same side as #1)Center of the room, then their sectorShort of #1, about 3 feet in from the entry wall
#4Opposite of #3 (same side as #2)Center of the room, then their sectorShort of #2, mirroring #3

Rules that hold every time

  • Don't stop in the doorway. Clear it in one motion.
  • Clear your immediate threat area first, then move along the wall.
  • #3 and #4 stop short so they don't mask #1 and #2.
  • If #1 has to go the "wrong" way, #2 adapts and goes opposite.
  • If the room is too small for everyone ("short room"), the last members hold outside.

Sectors

  • Each member's sector runs from their own wall across the room, ending about one arm's length short of the next teammate.
  • Sectors overlap in the room's depth, but no sector ever points at a teammate. #3 and #4 cover the center.
  • Weapons drop or lift off teammates as they enter a sector, and come back once they're past.

10Hallways and intersections

Hallways are long, narrow danger areas with doors along both sides. Keep spacing, keep every opening covered, and move steadily rather than fast.

Hallway: post an open door, then clear it

Hallway formations

Serpentine (single file, staggered): narrow halls. #1 covers forward, #2 high and forward, #3 and #4 alternate sides, last man covers rear.

Two-by-two: wide halls. Lead pair on opposite walls, staggered, both covering forward. #3 follows with muzzle off the lead; #4 covers the rear. Nobody covers across a teammate's lane.

Doors in the hall

  • The member on the same wall as an open door posts at the door edge and covers into the room. That sector points into the room, so teammates can pass in the hall without crossing it.
  • Don't cover a door from across the hall. Anyone who walks past ends up in that sector.
  • Clear the room or leave a member posted on it. Closed doors you aren't clearing get marked and reported.
  • Don't leave uncleared, uncovered doors behind the team.
T-intersection

T-intersections

#1 and #2 each take the branch on their own side and end back to back, so their sectors point away from each other. Don't cross over: crossing puts each lead's sector through the other's path. #3 moves up behind with muzzle down, #4 stays on rear security. The leader picks the direction; the other branch stays covered until the team has passed.

L-turns

Slice the corner from the outside wall. The lead uses the corner as cover and the team follows on the same side. Avoid bunching up at the corner while the lead works it.

11Stairs and multi-floor

  • Move up in a file with the lead covering the next landing and the member behind covering higher, across the open side of the stairwell.
  • Treat each landing as a corner: slice it before committing.
  • Clear stairs going up whenever possible. Coming down, your feet are exposed before your eyes can see the floor below.
  • Secure the top of the stairs as a foothold before the team spreads out on the new floor, and leave someone holding the stairwell.
  • On each floor, clear outward from the foothold room by room and report the floor clear before moving up.

12Consolidation and commands

After each room

  • Each member calls their sector; #1 or the leader calls "room clear."
  • Search for hidden spaces, closets, other doors, and connecting openings.
  • Mark the room per SOP: chalk, tape, chem light, or marker panel. Mark the door, not the inside wall.
  • Report to the leader: room cleared, casualties, anything found.

After the building

  • Establish security on likely approaches and overlooking positions.
  • Redistribute ammunition and equipment; account for every member.
  • Treat casualties and request evacuation.
  • Report building clear and mark it so other elements can see it from the street.
  • Prepare to continue to the next building or hold.

Verbal commands

Short, loud, and identical every time. Repeat a command back so the sender knows it was heard. These are common examples; your SOP sets the exact words.

Part II

Vehicle movement

Assessing a route, planning the move, placing the team in the vehicle, avoiding trouble, and reacting when you can't avoid it. The same rule as on foot applies: no sector covers a teammate or a friendly vehicle.

13Vehicle route assessment

A route assessment breaks the drive into segments and rates each one for how much it restricts you. You're looking for the places where you'll be forced to slow down, stop, or follow a single predictable path, since those are where trouble is easiest to set up.

What to look for

Choke pointsBridges, tunnels, narrow streets, construction, rail crossings, gated entrancesSingle path, no way around, often forced to slow
Forced stopsSignal lights, stop signs, toll booths, checkpoints, school zonesPredictable location where a vehicle sits still
Overlooking terrainOverpasses, high ground, buildings with a view of the road, wood lines close to the shoulderPlaces someone can observe or act from with an advantage
Canalizing terrainRoad cuts, embankments, walls, guard rails, mediansCan't leave the road to turn around or go around
Limited visibilityBlind curves, hill crests, heavy vegetation, poor lightingLess warning; shorter decision time

Running the assessment

  • Drive the route at the same time of day and day of week you'll use it, in a vehicle that blends in.
  • Record mileage and drive time for each segment, and mark each hazard by mile marker or landmark.
  • Photograph or video choke points and turnaround options.
  • Rate each segment LOW MODERATE HIGH and plan an alternate around every high-risk segment.

Establish the baseline

Know what normal looks like on this route so you notice changes.

  • Absence of the normal: an empty street that's usually busy, closed shops at midday, no kids at a school bus stop.
  • Presence of the abnormal: new debris or a parked vehicle where there never is one, people watching traffic instead of going about their business, signals between people.

14Vehicle route planning

The plan turns the assessment into a route set, timings, reporting points, and actions everyone has rehearsed before the wheels turn.

PACE routes

RouteUse
PPrimaryBest balance of time and risk under normal conditions
AAlternateBypasses the primary's high-risk segments; used if the primary is blocked or compromised
CContingencyDifferent direction of approach entirely; used if both primary and alternate are unusable
EEmergencyShortest path to the nearest safe haven or medical facility from any point on the route

Control measures

  • Start point / release point where the move begins and ends
  • Checkpoints at known landmarks for position reports
  • Rally points along the route where separated vehicles regroup
  • Decision points before each high-risk segment, where you choose primary or alternate
  • Safe havens marked with the route to each

Timing and pattern

  • Vary departure times and routes. Predictability is the biggest vulnerability on a recurring move.
  • Plan to arrive with daylight to spare when possible.
  • Avoid peak traffic through high-risk segments.
  • Set a "no later than" time at each checkpoint. A missed report triggers the lost-contact plan.

Interval and order of march

  • In towns, keep vehicles close enough that outside traffic can't cut between them, typically a few car lengths.
  • On open road, open the interval so one ambush or one blast can't catch two vehicles at once, while staying in sight and radio range.
  • Put the vehicle with the best observation and communications in the lead. The leader rides where they can see and control, usually not in the lead vehicle.
  • The trail vehicle watches the rear and reports anything that follows.

Movement brief checklist

Route

Strip map, PACE routes, checkpoints, rally points, safe havens, decision points, timings.

Actions

Halts, breakdowns, disabled vehicle, separation, contact front/left/right/rear, blocked route, bail-out.

Comms

Frequencies or channels, call signs, check-in times, lost-comms plan, code words.

Medical

Who carries the kit, casualty collection point, nearest hospitals on each route.

Vehicles

Order of march, seat assignments, sectors, load plan, fuel, spares, recovery gear.

Contingency

Who takes over if the leader is down, what triggers a route change, where to go if followed.

Vehicle prep

  • Fuel tank full at the start; refuel before it gets to half.
  • Tires, spare, jack, recovery strap, and jumper cables checked.
  • Medical kit within reach of the seats, not buried in cargo.
  • Paper map of the route as a backup to phones and GPS.
  • Loose gear secured so it doesn't become a projectile in a crash or hard turn.

15In-vehicle movement and team placement

Every seat has a job and a sector. Sectors divide the space around the vehicle so every direction is watched and no one's sector runs across another occupant.

SeatPrimary jobSectorOn contact
DriverDrive. Nothing else.Front, front-left, mirrorsKeeps the vehicle moving and out of the kill zone; follows the VC's direction
Vehicle cmdrNavigation, comms, decisionsFront, front-rightCalls direction, distance, and action ("contact left, push through" or "bail right")
Rear leftObservationLeft side to left rearReports, then exits away from contact on the bail-out call
Rear rightObservation, often medic or comms backupRight side to right rearSame as rear left; first out on a right-side bail

Riding

  • Seatbelts on. A crash is the most likely way to get hurt in a vehicle.
  • Weapons, if carried, stay muzzle down toward the floor or outboard, never toward another seat.
  • Doors locked. Bags, kits, and radios secured so they don't fly.
  • Talk is short and about the route. Call what you see by clock direction: "Vehicle stopped, two o'clock, 200 feet."

In traffic

  • Tires and pavement. At a stop, leave enough space that you can see the rear tires of the vehicle ahead touching the road. That leaves room to pull out around it.
  • Take the lane with the most escape options, usually not the curb lane in town.
  • At lights, time your approach to keep rolling when you can.
  • In a multi-vehicle move, the trail vehicle can take the lane beside the lead at a stop to prevent a box-in.

Bail-out drill

  1. The vehicle commander calls direction and exit side: "Contact left, bail right."
  2. Unbuckle, open the door away from contact, exit low.
  3. Occupants on the contact side cross the cab and exit with the others. Nobody steps out into the fire.
  4. Go to the engine block and wheels, not the doors (section 17).
  5. Sound off by seat or number so the commander knows everyone is out.

16Avoidance

Most bad outcomes on a route are avoidable by not being predictable, noticing early, keeping space, and acting decisively. Turning around early is almost always cheaper than reacting late.

Recognizing a blocked route and turning away

Indicators

What you seePossible meaning
Road blocked with no one working the sceneThe block is the point, not an accident
People standing off the road watching traffic, with no reason to be thereObservers or a trigger team
Normal activity suddenly stops or the area emptiesLocals know something is about to happen
The same vehicle behind you across several turnsSurveillance
A vehicle slows in front of you while another closes behindBox-in
New objects on the shoulder or in a culvert on a road you drive regularlySomething placed for your route

At choke points

  • Scan the choke point and the far side before you enter it.
  • Slow early rather than stop late. A rolling vehicle has more options than a stopped one.
  • Leave an escape gap ahead and to one side.
  • In a multi-vehicle move, the trail vehicle holds short until the lead is through.
  • Know your turnaround point before you commit to the segment.

If you think you're followed

  • Don't go home or to your destination.
  • Confirm with a few deliberate, normal-looking turns. A vehicle that stays with you through all of them is a strong indicator.
  • Report it, then drive to a safe haven: a police or fire station, or a busy staffed location.
  • Note plate, make, model, color, occupants, and time. Don't confront.

Turning away

Pick your decision points and turnaround options during planning, so the choice on the road is just which one to use. In a two-vehicle move, the trail vehicle turns first and becomes the lead. Report what you saw and where as soon as you're clear.

17Vehicle route engagements

If contact can't be avoided, the vehicle is your best tool for getting out of it. The core principle is to get off the X: leave the place where you were attacked as fast as the vehicle allows.

Drive through the kill zone
SituationAction
Contact while mobile, vehicle can moveDrive through. Don't stop in the kill zone. Rally past it.
Vehicles short of the kill zoneDon't drive in. Stop, report, and take the alternate to the rally point. Support only if you can do it without entering.

Cover at the vehicle

Disabled vehicle: bail out, cover, bound to hard cover
  • Get behind the engine block first. Behind the wheels is second best. Keep your feet behind the wheels too; rounds skip under vehicles.
  • Stay a step or two back from the vehicle rather than pressed against it. It gives you room to move and keeps you out of rounds that skim along the body.
  • The vehicle is temporary cover. Fuel, glass, and a stationary target make it a poor place to stay. Move to hard cover as soon as you can.
  • Pairs bound in fire-and-movement. Movers' routes stay out of the covering pair's sectors.

Security at halts

Herringbone halt

Herringbone

Used on a road when the halt is short or the route must stay open. Vehicles angle off alternately left and right, crews facing outward. A vehicle can still pass down the center.

Coil

Used for longer halts off the road. Vehicles form a circle with fronts or weapons outward and soft vehicles and people in the center. Takes more space and time to set.

5 and 25 checks

Before anyone gets out at a halt, the crew searches about 15 feet around the vehicle from inside, looking for anything disturbed or out of place. Then, on foot, they check out to about 80 feet. Only after the area is checked does the rest of the team dismount. The names come from the doctrinal 5- and 25-meter distances.

After contact

  1. Rally past the kill zone or at the planned rally point.
  2. Set security with sectors outward.
  3. Account for every person and vehicle.
  4. Treat casualties and request evacuation.
  5. Report: location, what happened, casualties, vehicle status.
  6. Check vehicles for damage: tires, fluids, glass, steering.
  7. Decide: continue, alternate route, or return.

19Training progression

Crawl

Walk-through

Tape a floor plan on the ground or use an empty building. Walk each position slowly, call each step out loud, and mark points of domination. No weapons or training weapons only.

Walk

Dry rehearsal

Full speed with training weapons or inert replicas. Add doors, furniture, a second room, and role players. Add low light once daylight is clean.

Run

Force on force or live

Only in an approved facility, with qualified range staff, a written safety plan, and a team that has passed the earlier stages. Marking cartridges before live fire.

  • Run each drill with every member in every position. Anyone may end up #1.
  • Change the floor plan often. A team that knows the shoot house is rehearsing the house, not the drill.
  • After-action review after every iteration: what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, what to sustain, what to fix.
  • Recon training pairs well with the land navigation and observation drills you can run anywhere: KIM games, range estimation, and sketching a building from across the street.
